KYOTO CITY KYOCERA MUSEUM OF ART: Kyoto, Japan
ID: kyoto-city-kyocera-museum-of-art-kyoto
Website: https://kyotocity-kyocera.museum/ (checked 2026-10-24)

OVERVIEW
- Municipal art museum at 124 Okazaki Enshoji-cho, Sakyo-ku, Kyoto, in the Okazaki district, with an annex at 13 Okazaki Saishoji-cho (the museum's visit page).
- Opened in 1933 as the Kyoto Enthronement Memorial Museum of Art, built to mark the 1928 enthronement ceremonies; renamed the Kyoto Municipal Museum of Art after the Second World War (the museum's history page).
- Named since 2020 for Kyocera Corporation under a naming-rights contract (see Funding).
- Building: designed by Maeda Kenjiro through an open competition. The museum closed in 2017, building work ran from January 2018 to October 2019, and Jun Aoki & Associates and Tezzo Nishizawa Architects, selected in 2015 from 19 applicants, kept the historic main building and added Higashiyama Cube, a new building for contemporary art. It reopened on 26 May 2020.
- On site: main building exhibition rooms, collection rooms and Higashiyama Cube.
- Visiting: 10:00 to 18:00, last admission 17:30; closed Monday (open when Monday is a public holiday) and 28 December to 2 January. The pages read list no admission fees or accessibility details.

FUNDING
Kyoto City KYOCERA Museum of Art: municipal, with a corporate naming-rights payment and small corporate memberships
- Kyocera Corporation agreed to pay 5 billion yen (excluding tax) for 50 years of naming rights, May 2020 to May 2070. It was paid in lump sums: 1.5 billion yen in fiscal 2017, 1.5 billion in fiscal 2018 and 2 billion in fiscal 2019 (Kyoto City naming-rights page, April 2026).
- The renovation cost 11.1 billion yen, and Kyocera's payment covers about half (Kyoto Minpo, 22 November 2019).
- Kyoto City sold the naming rights (the city's naming-rights page). No reporting found of the museum's yearly operating budget on the pages read.
- Corporate membership: 19 supporters, among them the Inamori Foundation, the Nissha Foundation, Kyoto Bank, Secom and Ippodo Tea. The list carries no year or amounts.
- No reporting found of the source of the Inamori Foundation's money.

Red flags: naming-rights sale and protest
- In 2017 Kishi Kaske, representative of the Kyoto City Museum of Art Issue Study Group, argued that a company name signals weak cultural standing, that calling it a "nickname" avoids council approval, and that donors who gave about 80 percent of the collection were disrespected. The group asked Kyocera and its founder Kazuo Inamori to decline, and sought a withdrawal of the deal (artmovement.jp, 8 July 2017, an advocacy site of the Japan Art Association).
- On 16 November 2019, at the completion ceremony, about 15 citizens and artists, including the sculptor Kishi Kaske, protested outside under the slogan "Give back the museum's name" (my translation). The group was the Kyoto Water and Green Protection Network (Kyoto Minpo; my translation of the group's name).
- Kyoto Minpo calls it the first public art museum in Japan to sell naming rights, and says citizens and arts figures criticised the deal.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Criticism is documented but sits in a Japanese art-advocacy site and local press, not in international art magazines; none found in English-language art media.
1. artmovement.jp (Japan Art Association): Kishi Kaske, "The problem of museum naming rights tests the cultural level of the Japanese" (2017, my translation of the title)
URL: https://www.artmovement.jp/2017/07/08/144-028/
- Argues a 4-year mayoral term cannot legitimately bind a museum for 50 years, that corporate perks such as an advertising booth risk privatising the museum and pressuring artists, and that the yearly payment is low.
- Names Kyoto City, its mayor, Kyocera and Inamori. Written by a campaigner, not a critic.
2. Kyoto Minpo: "Give back the museum's name" (2019, my translation)
URL: https://www.kyoto-minpo.net/archives/2019/11/22/post-24056.php
- Reports the ceremony-day protest and the 11.1 billion yen cost. A local weekly; its stance is not stated and its page carries a link to the Japanese Communist Party.

BOTTOM LINE
The money is mostly public, with one large corporate payment that bought the name and is stated openly by the city. The weak points are the naming-rights sale itself, which citizens and artists contested from 2016 to 2019, and the lack of published accounts on the pages read. Watch the corporate membership list and any further naming deals.
