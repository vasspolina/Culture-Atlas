MANGYONGDAE CHILDREN'S PALACE: Pyongyang, North Korea
ID: mangyongdae-children-s-palace
Website: none (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- After-school palace (만경대학생소년궁전) on Kwangbok (Liberation) Street in the north of Mangyongdae District, Pyongyang; Koryo Tours (January 2019) and the Ministry of Unification call it the largest of North Korea's schoolchildren's palaces.
- Built from 5 September 1986 and completed on 2 May 1989 (Ministry of Unification), ahead of the 13th World Festival of Youth and Students (Koryo Tours).
- A central building with two wings, said to resemble a mother's outstretched arms, stands behind a sculpture group and two fountains 90 and 100 metres high (Koryo Tours). The Ministry of Unification gives it 270 m long, 190 m wide and 60 m high, with 103,000 square metres of floor space and room for 12,000 children a day.
- It has 120 rooms, a swimming pool, a gymnasium, a library and a 2,000-seat theatre (Koryo Tours); the ministry counts about 200 club and activity rooms and outdoor rooms for car and tractor driving, computing and astronomy.
- Small groups learn music, dance, foreign languages, science and computing, traditional arts and sports (Koryo Tours). The ministry says about 5,000 Pyongyang pupils come each day, taught by about 500 full-time staff with university staff as visiting teachers; Wikipedia, citing The Telegraph, counts over 500 teachers.
- Renovation: a completion ceremony for the refit was held on 8 December 2015, after KCNA reported on 1 December that Kim Jong Un had toured the refitted palace; Jon Yong Nam, chairman of the Kim Il Sung Socialist Youth League central committee, gave the speech (Yonhap, from North Korean radio).
- The palace's art troupe performed in Russia's Primorsky Krai, in Vladivostok and Artyom, from 2 to 12 May 2025 (Ministry of Unification, from Rodong Sinmun and KCNA).
- Koryo Tours says performances are held every Tuesday and Thursday, that foreigners can attend, and that most of its trips include the palace.

FUNDING
Mangyongdae Children's Palace: state-run: a public facility of the North Korean state and its children's and youth organisations
- Manager: Koryo Tours says the Korean Children's Union manages the palace; Wikipedia names the Korean Youth Corps instead. Both call it a public facility.
- Leader's gifts: Rodong Sinmun wrote in November 2025 that Kim Jong Un, visiting on 30 November 2015, sent modern teaching equipment, musical instruments and sports gear (SPN, from Rodong Sinmun). The Ministry of Unification lists a delivery of modern teaching equipment in October 2016 among his activities.
- Staff: the ministry's 2022 directory lists Ryom Yun Hak (렴윤학) as president (총장), with an arts vice-president and three vice-presidents (my romanisation).
- State claims: Rodong Sinmun said that in the ten years after the refit 1.32 million pupils took part in its activities (SPN).
- North Korea announces its state budget as yearly percentage changes, not amounts. KCNA claimed on 24 March 2026 that 2026 spending would reach 105.8 percent of 2025, with funds for the educational sector at 106 percent of last year's.

Red flags: US sanctions on the North Korean government, 2016
- US Executive Order 13722 of 15 March 2016 blocks property in US jurisdiction of the Government of North Korea and the Workers' Party of Korea; section 9 defines the Government as the DPRK government and its agencies, instrumentalities and controlled entities (Federal Register, 18 March 2016). The order does not name the palace.

Not related: the Pyongyang Children's Palace, a separate palace north of Kim Il Sung Square, founded in 1963 (Wikipedia).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: One news photo report, not art criticism, covers a 2016 gala staged at the palace for foreign visitors.
1. NBC News: Damir Sagolj (Reuters photographs), "North Korean Children Perform Song and Dance for Foreign Press" (2016)
URL: https://www.nbcnews.com/slideshow/north-korean-children-perform-song-dance-foreign-press-n568891
- A gala was held at the palace on 5 May 2016 for visiting foreign journalists and tourists, ahead of the Seventh Workers' Party Congress.
- NBC says many of the orchestral, choir and acrobatic performances carried political undertones, and that foreign reporters' movements were closely managed.

BOTTOM LINE
The palace is paid for by the North Korean state, and the US blocking order on that state is its one Red flag.
