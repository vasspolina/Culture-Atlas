LIU MANOR MUSEUM (RENT COLLECTION COURTYARD): Dayi, China
ID: liu-manor-museum-rent-collection-courtyard
Website: none (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Museum in the former manor of the landlord Liu Wencai (1887 to 1949) at 15 Jingui Street, Anren town, Dayi county, Chengdu, Sichuan; the site covers over 70,000 square metres with more than 540 rooms (Dahe Piao ticket guide, 2025).
- Opened as an exhibition hall in 1959, in answer to the Ministry of Culture's call for a museum in every county (Ho and Li); a 2025 ticket guide dates the exhibition hall to October 1958.
- Names: Landlord Manor Exhibition Hall (1959), Class Education Exhibition Hall (1966), Landlord Manor Museum (1980) and today's Liu Family Manor Museum (Ho and Li, citing the Dayi county gazetteer). Ho and Li describe it as a nationally ranked cultural relic.
- The Rent Collection Courtyard, 114 life-sized clay figures showing Liu's tenants paying rent, was made in 1965 by Wang Guanyi and students of the Sichuan Fine Arts Institute and still stands behind glass in the manor (Smarthistory, Ho and Li). In the Cultural Revolution it was hailed as an "artistic atom bomb", with replicas shown in major cities and abroad (Ho and Li).
- Ho and Li write that the museum received tens of millions of visitors in the Mao era.
- In 1999 Cai Guo-Qiang restaged the work for the Venice Biennale and won the Golden Lion; some of the original sculptors threatened a copyright suit, and Smarthistory names Luo Zhongli, president of the Sichuan Fine Arts Institute, as threatening to sue the Biennale (Ho and Li, Smarthistory).
- The collection holds more than 20,000 objects, over 2,700 of them graded as precious (Dahe Piao).
- Visiting: open daily 9.00 to 17.00, 8.30 to 18.00 on public holidays; adults 40 yuan, students 20 yuan, free for under-16s, over-65s and disabled visitors (Dahe Piao, 2025).

FUNDING
Liu Manor Museum: state-run: county government administration
- Origin: the manor and Liu's movable property were seized after 1949; furniture and goods went to government offices and the county Party committee, and the house became the museum (Ho and Li).
- Administration: after visitors and ticket sales fell sharply from 1978, the museum was demoted to county-level administration in 1984 (Ho and Li).
- Changes to the displays needed higher approval: in 1988, with provincial permission, staff turned the water dungeon into an "opium storage room" and painted over the "bloody handprints" (Ho and Li).
- In the 1980s staff raised extra money by showing films, staging operas and fashion shows, hosting a martial arts school and opening a zoo (Ho and Li).

Not related: the Jianchuan Museum Cluster across the street, a private museum complex founded in 2005 by the real estate developer and collector Fan Jianchuan (Ho and Li). It is a separate institution.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: The main critique is academic: a 2016 study of how the museum manufactured its story of landlord cruelty.
1. Modern China: Denise Y. Ho and Jie Li, "From Landlord Manor to Red Memorabilia: Reincarnations of a Chinese Museum Town" (2016)
URL: https://deniseyho.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/holi_landlordmanor_2016.pdf
- At the height of the Mao years the authorities fabricated evidence such as the water dungeon, and "witnesses" gave false testimony that moved millions of listeners.
- In the Cultural Revolution the museum and the Rent Collection Courtyard served as sites for show trials of newly denounced "class enemies".
- Staff furnished period rooms with substitutes, including a gilded "dragon bed" made by local carpenters.
- Records the counter-story of Liu Wencai's grandson Liu Xiaofei, who calls the displays fabricated history; an academic journal, not an art outlet.

BOTTOM LINE
Public money under county administration, with no outside funder of concern. The weak point is its origin as a state propaganda site.
