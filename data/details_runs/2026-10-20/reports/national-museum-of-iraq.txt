NATIONAL MUSEUM OF IRAQ: Baghdad, Iraq
ID: national-museum-of-iraq
Website: https://theiraqmuseum.com/ (checked 2026-10-24; working, but ownership by the museum is not stated, the footer reads 2020 and it gives no hours or prices)

OVERVIEW
- Iraq's national archaeological museum in the Al-Salihiyyah district of Baghdad, with 28 galleries and vaults of Mesopotamian, Islamic and pre-Islamic Arabian objects (Wikipedia).
- Gertrude Bell began collecting finds in Baghdad in 1922; the collection became the Baghdad Antiquities Museum on 14 June 1926 with Bell as director. In 1966 it moved to a two-storey building of about 45,000 square metres and was renamed the Iraq Museum (Wikipedia).
- Looting after the 2003 invasion removed about 15,000 items, including 5,000 cylinder seals, and 4,300 had been returned at the time of UNESCO's 2015 statement (UNESCO).
- It reopened on 28 February 2015 and holds artefacts dating back 7,000 years (UNESCO).
- No reporting found of current opening days, admission or accessibility on the pages read.

FUNDING
National Museum of Iraq: public with foreign grants: Iraqi antiquities ministry, plus US, Italian and Japanese money for restoration
- Run through Iraq's ministry for tourism and antiquities; in 2009 it was Minister Qahtan al-Juburi who set the reopening date (Archaeology).
- The US announced a 13 million dollar grant on 20 October 2008, mainly for restoring the museum, announced by Goli Ameri of the State Department (RFE/RL).
- By 2011 the deputy director, Mohsen Hassan Ali, said "most of the money" had come from the US, about 9 million dollars; Italians renovated the main hall (Christian Science Monitor).
- UNESCO (2 March 2015): "Thanks to funding from Japan and Italy, the museum laboratory was restored and specialized training for staff provided."
- In October 2019 Minister Abdulameer al-Hamdani announced a planned national museum complex to replace it, which The Art Newspaper reported foreign oil companies would fund; he named none and gave no cost.
- No reporting found of Italy equipping the museum's laboratories in 2015 beyond UNESCO's statement above, of a show sponsored by France, or of an annual budget, donors or board.

Red flags: archaeologists' protest over the 2009 reopening
- January 2009: Minister al-Juburi wanted the museum open by mid-February; Amira Edan, acting head of the State Board of Antiquities and Heritage, opposed the plan, and al-Juburi fired her from that post (Archaeology, 22 January 2009).
- 2009: five Iraqi archaeologists wrote to Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki calling a sudden reopening plan a "remarkable unawareness of cultural heritage management". Donny George Youkhanna said it went against the wishes of director Edan and her staff (Museums Journal).
- Museums Journal (13 November 2009) reported that Edan had been removed the previous month as chair of the State Board of Antiquities and stayed on as museum director.

- The ministry did not respond to the newspaper's request for comment.

WIDER CONTEXT
- After the 2003 invasion the museum was left largely unguarded and looted while the Ministry of Oil received special protection (Christian Science Monitor); US forces faced criticism for not stopping the looting (RFE/RL).
- The museum is the building that was looted, and the US later provided most of its restoration money (Christian Science Monitor).

BOTTOM LINE
The museum's money is state money plus named foreign governments, the US, Italy and Japan, with no corporate sponsor found. The weak points are a ministry that overrode its own antiquities staff on reopening in 2009, and an unnamed oil-company funding plan for a replacement museum. The museum publishes no accounts, so watch who pays for the new complex.
