HEROES' ACRE (WINDHOEK): Windhoek, Namibia
ID: heroes-acre-windhoek
Website: none

OVERVIEW
- National war memorial and burial ground in uninhabited hills 10 kilometres south of central Windhoek, on the B1 road to Rehoboth; it opened on 26 August 2002 (Wikipedia).
- Sam Nujoma conceived the idea in 1997 at an OAU summit in Harare (New Era, 2006). North Korea's Mansudae Overseas Projects designed and built it, and Van der Hoog calls it, like Zimbabwe's Acre, a copy of the Revolutionary Martyrs' Cemetery near Pyongyang (Leiden University thesis, 2024).
- The City of Windhoek donated more than 732 hectares of land, and Mansudae built the site in 13 months (Navigate Namibia).
- A marble obelisk and a bronze Unknown Soldier, parade grounds with a grandstand for 5,000 and 174 tombs, with cenotaphs for nine national heroes named at the inauguration (Wikipedia).
- Presidents Hage Geingob (buried February 2024, the first in the presidential mausoleums) and Sam Nujoma (state funeral on 1 March 2025) lie there (Wikipedia; New Era, 2025).
- Visitors: 7,533 people from April 2005 to March 2006 against an expected 20,000 (New Era, 2006). In 2017 school and university groups made up most Namibian visitors, international tourists most daily ones, and soldiers guarded the site around the clock (New Era, 2017).
- Visiting: for 1 April 2024 to 30 April 2025 the National Heritage Council set admission at N$50 for Namibian adults self-guided and N$60 guided, N$70 and N$80 for international adults, N$10 per learner on school tours, with young children free (Government Gazette No. 8206).

FUNDING
Heroes' Acre (Windhoek): public: Government of Namibia paid for the build; the National Heritage Council runs it
- The Acre was budgeted at N$34 million and built for N$60 million (Van der Hoog, p.147). New Era (2006) also gives the N$60 million cost.
- Running it: in 2006 admission fees raised N$64,188 against upkeep of N$500,000 a year, described as a drain on the National Monuments Council's meagre finances (Van der Hoog, p.147).
- The National Heritage Council, the statutory body under the National Heritage Act 2004, sets the fees with the minister's approval (Government Gazette No. 8206, 4 September 2023) and administers the site (Navigate Namibia). The Ministry of Works and Transport has the mandate of keeping it up to standard (New Era, 2017); in 2006 its under secretary said the site had gone three years without maintenance (New Era, 2006).
- State funerals: state and official funerals, including those of Geingob and Nujoma at Heroes' Acre, cost the government N$38.4 million in 2024/25, according to Prime Minister Elijah Ngurare (New Era, 28 April 2025).

Red flags: 2002 cost overrun and closed tendering on the Mansudae contract
- Insight Namibia's Corruption Tracker (March 2008) said Mansudae's Heroes' Acre contract ended up costing twice as much as first expected, and that its State House contract went out with no competitive tender.
- Van der Hoog records the rise from N$34 million to N$60 million and says African governments usually award Mansudae projects by closed tender, so total payments cannot be determined (pp.147 to 148).

Red flags: builder Mansudae sanctioned by the US, UN and EU (2016 to 2017)
- The UN Panel of Experts (S/2017/150, table 4) lists the Hero's Acre among Mansudae's works in Namibia. Para 118 recommends designating Mansudae for arms-related services, building the Oamites munitions factory on behalf of KOMID, and bulk cash transfers by its workers; para 117 says services for the maintenance of North Korean statues are also prohibited.
- OFAC blocked Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies on 2 December 2016 (Federal Register); the UN Security Council designated it in Resolution 2371 of 5 August 2017, and the EU followed, naming Namibia among its countries of business (EU Regulation 2017/1457).
- Namibia announced in June 2016 that it would end the services of KOMID and Mansudae (S/2017/150, para 112). On 17 November 2017 it told the Panel that all North Korean workers had left and supplied cancellation orders for Mansudae projects (S/2018/171, paras 104 to 106).
- Van der Hoog notes the 2006 sanctions regime did not criminalise construction work (p.148).

Not related: the National Heroes' Acre near Harare, Zimbabwe, a separate site built by the same contractor and inaugurated in 1982 (Van der Hoog).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Criticism concerns money and contracting, not the design.
1. Insight Namibia: Corruption Tracker, "The hidden world of public contracting" (2008)
URL: https://web.archive.org/web/20110722234221/http://www.hsf.org.na/download/TrackerMarch08.pdf
- Names the Heroes' Acre contract as costing twice the first estimate and the State House award to Mansudae as untendered.
- Criticises procurement, not the monument.
2. Leiden University: Tycho van der Hoog, "North Korea and the Liberation of Southern Africa, 1960-2020", chapter "Money: Nationalism as a Business Model" (2024)
URL: https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/download/1887/3753571/1
- North Korean projects tend to cost much more than planned; gives the Acre's budget, cost and upkeep.
- Criticises the contracting, not the design.

BOTTOM LINE
Namibian public money built and runs the Acre, and no flagged funder pays for it. The weak points are the overrun on a closed-tender contract and the payments to a builder later sanctioned for arms work in Namibia.
