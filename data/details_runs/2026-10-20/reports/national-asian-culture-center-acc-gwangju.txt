NATIONAL ASIAN CULTURE CENTER (ACC): Gwangju, South Korea
ID: national-asian-culture-center-acc-gwangju
Website: https://www.acc.go.kr

OVERVIEW
- State-run arts complex, also called the Asia Culture Center, at 38 Munhwajeondang-ro, Dong-gu, Gwangju; it belongs to the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (the centre's own site).
- Built between 2008 and 2014 under a plan finalised in 2007 (the centre's own site); it opened in November 2015 (Wikipedia). Seoul Shinmun dated the partial opening to 4 September 2015.
- Seoul Shinmun (2015) says the complex was built on the site of the former South Jeolla Provincial Office. Wikipedia says the final design kept the above-ground buildings and added underground ones, and that the centre pays tribute to the 1980 Gwangju Uprising.
- Facilities: ACC Archive & Research, the Asia Culture Museum, ACC Creation, ACC Theater, ACC Children and, since May 2026, ACC Culture Exchange (the centre's site).
- The centre first operated as two bodies, the government-run Culture Center and the Asia Culture Institute, a corporation that ran the centre under entrustment and began operating on 1 October 2015 (Seoul Shinmun, Choi Chi-bong, 2 October 2015). They were merged into one integrated centre in January 2022 (Seoul Shinmun, Seo Mi-ae, 3 May 2024). The Ministry announced on 17 January 2022 the launch of a National Asia Culture Center Foundation, responsible for activating the centre's operation.
- Indoor facilities open 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., closed Mondays and New Year's Day; outdoor areas 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. The Archive & Research, the Asia Culture Museum, the Play Library and Play Kids are free; other facilities charge by programme.

FUNDING
National Asian Culture Center (ACC): mainly public: South Korea's Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism
- Seoul Shinmun (Choi Chi-bong, 2 October 2015) reported that 703 billion won was spent on the ACC project, with construction from June 2008 to October 2014.
- Korea Times (2008) reported the wider Gwangju hub project, then costed at 5.3 trillion won, was to be financed 52.8 percent by the government and the rest by the private sector and local administration.
- Newsis (25 October 2018) reported 211 staff, 61 at the Culture Center and 150 at the Asia Culture Institute.
- Munhwa Ilbo (14 August 2020) reported an amendment to the Asia Culture Special Act sponsored by Rep. Lee Byung-hun: it drops the plan to turn the Culture Center into a corporation and makes it a permanent agency under the Ministry, and creates a separate culture foundation for revenue work such as children's experience facilities, content distribution and tourism products.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: The criticism comes from news coverage, not the art press, and concerns cost and governance.
1. The Korea Times: Kang Hyun-kyung, "Culture Hub Plan Losing Money" (2008)
URL: https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/20081021/culture-hub-plan-losing-money
- Reports Rep. Lee Bum-rae citing a Seoul National University study projecting an annual shortfall of about 100 billion won on the 5.3 trillion won Gwangju hub project, and arguing public funds should not go to a plan that would "generate massive losses in the future".
- Concerns the whole 5.3 trillion won Gwangju hub project, which includes the Asia Culture Complex; published seven years before the centre opened.
2. Newsis: Ryu Hyeong-geun, "Also Discussed at Gwangju City Parliamentary Audit... Seeking Ways to Revitalize the Asia Culture Center" (25 October 2018, title translated)
URL: https://mobile.newsis.com/view/NISX20181025_0000453824
- Reports lawmaker Ju Seung-yong saying that the Culture Center paid 11 billion won in January 2016 after losing part of a damages suit by four firms including Daelim Industrial over construction delays; the delays were linked to protests by May 18 groups that demanded preservation of the former provincial office annex.
- Ju said the split between the Culture Center (61 staff) and the Asia Culture Institute (150 staff) caused duplicated work and inefficient performance management.
- Dated before the January 2022 merger.

WIDER CONTEXT
- Wikipedia says the choice of Gwangju was disputed because the city has no historic links to heritage sites or cultural significance related to art, that construction was delayed for several years by complaints from the local community, and that residents, families of victims and May 18 Democratization Movement organisations felt the project did not honour the original promises of preserving the movement's legacy.
- Connection to the centre: Seoul Shinmun says the complex was built on the former South Jeolla Provincial Office site, and Newsis says May 18 groups demanded preservation of its annex.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the centre is public: the South Korean government paid for the building and runs the centre through the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, with a foundation set up in January 2022 to support its operation. The documented criticism concerns cost, duplicated governance before the 2022 merger and, per Wikipedia, the handling of the former provincial office site.
