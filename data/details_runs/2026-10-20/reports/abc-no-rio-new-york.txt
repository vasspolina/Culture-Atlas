ABC NO RIO: New York, USA
ID: abc-no-rio-new-york
Website: https://www.abcnorio.org (checked 2026-10-25)

OVERVIEW
- Volunteer-led, collectively run arts and activism centre at 156 Rivington Street on the Lower East Side of Manhattan. Its site calls it a "nonprofit community center for arts and activism" operating since 1980.
- Grew out of The Real Estate Show, organised by the artists' group Colab in late 1979 in an abandoned building at 123 Delancey Street; city officials closed the show within a day, and the organisers then negotiated the use of 156 Rivington Street (Wikipedia).
- Named after a partly burnt neon sign that once read "Abogado Con Notario"; only fragments remained (Wikipedia).
- Building: the old tenement, dating to the 1880s, was sold by the city to the group for $1 in 2006 and demolished in 2016 (Commercial Observer, 2024). The new four-storey building is designed by Paul A. Castrucci Architects to LEED Silver and Passive House standards (6sqft, 4 November 2025). Commercial Observer (22 August 2024) said the project had "finally kicked into gear last month"; 6sqft dates the start of construction to September 2024 and reports the topping out as 15 months later. The ABC No Rio site says construction is underway and the building is expected to be finished in fall 2026.
- What is there and planned: exhibitions, hardcore and punk matinees, the Comics as Political Expression programme, a zine library, printshop, darkroom and computer centre. The new building adds a gallery, a rooftop courtyard and meeting spaces (Commercial Observer).
- Visiting: the site lists no general hours or admission. While the building is unfinished the group is operating "in exile" at other venues such as P.I.T. in Brooklyn, with listed show tickets at $10 to $20 sliding scale. Accessibility is not stated on the pages read.

FUNDING
ABC No Rio: mainly public capital funding for the new building, plus donations and benefit income
- 6sqft (4 November 2025) calls the new building a $21 million project funded by the city through the Department of Cultural Affairs, the Manhattan borough president's office and City Council members. The Lower Manhattan Development Corporation and the New York State Council on the Arts also helped raise funds, and ABC No Rio itself contributed $1.7 million.
- Commercial Observer (22 August 2024) says capital funding came from the city's cultural affairs department and private donors, including a $1 million anonymous gift, and that the group had raised $21 million in total.
- Land: the city sold the building to the group for $1 on 29 June 2006; the buyer had to raise money for renovations and dedicate the building to community use (The Nation, 2008).
- Running programmes: the site lists donations, merchandise, "Art for Donations" and a brick fundraiser, and names no funders. No reporting found of corporate sponsors or of current grant amounts for programming.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Very little criticism. Coverage is mostly sympathetic; the one piece found raises, and answers, the question of whether rebuilding betrays the group's DIY ethics.
1. The Nation: Kevin Erickson, "Reclaiming New York for Local Arts: ABC No Rio" (10 January 2008)
URL: https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/reclaiming-new-york-local-arts-abc-no-rio/tnamp/
- Largely sympathetic account of the group's survival against displacement and gentrification, in a series by the All Ages Movement Project.
- After consulting architects and engineers the group decided to rebuild from scratch, at a cost approaching nearly $2 million. The piece raises the question of whether supporters see this as an abandonment of DIY ethics; it reports that the volunteers it spoke with felt that "the radical possibilities opened up by sustainability outweigh any attachment to the aesthetics of dilapidation."
- Reports that audience and programming have changed as gentrification pushed out the neighbourhood's residents. Not an art-press outlet.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the space shows no flagged funder: public arts funding, the city's land sale and private donations, with no fossil fuel, tobacco, arms or Gulf state funder found. The weak point is dependence on city money and a city land deal for the new building; Commercial Observer notes the group has been repairing its relationship with the city since a 1990s city standoff with squatters. Worth watching: whether the building opens in fall 2026 and whether programming funders are named.
