MANSUDAE ART STUDIO: Pyongyang, North Korea
ID: mansudae-art-studio-pyongyang
Website: https://korean-books.com.kp (checked 2026-10-24; fails the secure connection, certificate could not be verified)

OVERVIEW
- State-run art production complex in the Phyongchon District of Pyongyang, over 120,000 square metres, about 80,000 of them indoors (Wikipedia).
- Founded 17 November 1959. About 4,000 staff, roughly 1,000 of them artists, in 13 production groups (including woodcut, charcoal drawing, ceramics, embroidery and jewel painting) and more than 50 supply departments (Wikipedia).
- Its overseas arm, the Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies, was set up in the 1970s and has built monuments in African and Asian countries (Wikipedia).
- Abroad it has had a space in Beijing's 798 art district since 2009 (Wikipedia) and sold through the Italian dealer Pier Luigi Cecioni, who began cultivating the relationship in 2005 (CBS News, 2018).
- Visiting: Koryo Tours (guide of 7 February 2019) says the studio "is on the UN Sanction list and cannot be visited nor art purchased".

FUNDING
Mansudae Art Studio: state-run, earning foreign currency from art sales and overseas monument contracts
- State institution: CBS News calls it a state-run arts enterprise.
- Art sales: Vice (2013) estimated about $160 million earned over the previous decade and wrote that "all the art sales go straight to the state". Cecioni told Vice the studio has "a perhaps unexpected economic autonomy" and that sales money goes to the studio itself; he told Reuters the revenues pay for paints and equipment (Taipei Times, 2017).
- Monuments abroad: CBS News puts Africa monument revenue at an estimated $260 million as of 2016. A UN report said the overseas arm exported workers for construction, generating revenue for the North Korean government or the Workers' Party of Korea (CBS News, OpenSanctions).
- Buyers: Cecioni sells Mansudae works online and at fairs under what he calls an exclusive agreement; Dandong galleries sold paintings for up to $100,000, with mark-ups of four or five times (Reuters via Taipei Times, 2017).
- No reporting found of the studio's budget.

Red flags: UN, EU and US sanctions on the studio
- 2016: UN Security Council Resolution 2321 banned North Korean exports of statues (unscr.com); Reuters says the ban hit Mansudae's statue business (Taipei Times, 2017).
- 5 August 2017: the UN Security Council listed "Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies", alias Mansudae Art Studio, under the DPRK programme (UN consolidated list, as mirrored by Lursoft). CBS News reports a travel ban and a global asset freeze.
- EU: Commission Implementing Regulation 2017/1457 of 10 August 2017 lists the same company and alias, designation date 5 August 2017 (legislation.gov.uk).
- US: on 2 December 2016 the Treasury (OFAC) designated Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies under Executive Order 13722 for exporting workers from North Korea (US Treasury press release).
- November 2018: South Korean business executives on a Pyongyang visit bought paintings at the studio; customs seized about 20 at Incheon airport. The Unification Ministry said it would decide under related laws whether this breached sanctions (UPI, 2019).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Little direct criticism. One piece questions how a biennale sourced the studio's works.
1. Hyperallergic: Hayoung Chung, "Curators Strive to Transcend National Borders at the Gwangju Biennale" (2018)
URL: https://hyperallergic.com/imagined-borders-gwangju-biennale-2018
- Says wall labels and catalogue give only titles, artists' names and dates, leaving viewers little chance to understand the works.
- Says the Biennale Foundation has not explained sufficiently how it sourced the works and that Korean media questioned the authenticity and provenance of some pieces.
- Names Mansudae Art Studio; does not mention sanctions.
2. Hyperallergic: Zachary Small, "The Italian Gallery Brokering an International Market for North Korean Art" (2018)
URL: https://hyperallergic.com/442425/pier-luigi-cecioni-mansudae-studio/
- Reports that, according to the UN, Mansudae's profits go to North Korea's government, against Cecioni's claim that his payments "go back into running the studio". Says UN sanctions have limited what Cecioni can import and that he still has over 300 pieces in storage.
- Not criticism: it records Cecioni's statements without independent checks.
3. VICE: Nadja Sayej, "Behind Mansudae, the Most Prominent Art Institution in North Korea" (2013)
URL: https://vice.com/en/article/behind-mansudae-the-most-prominent-art-institution-in-north-korea
- Says artists get no return on sales and that all sales go to the state; Cecioni says the money stays with the studio. Interview-led profile, not a critique.

BOTTOM LINE
The studio is a state institution whose parent company is on UN, EU and US sanctions lists, and the UN says its overseas earnings go to the North Korean government or the Workers' Party. The weak points are the competing claims about where art-sale money goes, continued sales through dealers and the 2018 Gwangju loan of its works. Worth watching: further sales or loans to institutions abroad.
