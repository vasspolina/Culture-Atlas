OVERVIEW
- Public art gallery in downtown Winnipeg: the 1971 main building and Qaumajuq, a building for Inuit art opened in March 2021 (Wikipedia; The Art Newspaper).
- Its roots go back to the Winnipeg Museum of Fine Arts, opened on 16 December 1912; the present Winnipeg Art Gallery was incorporated by the Manitoba legislature on 6 May 1963 (Wikipedia).
- Qaumajuq is Inuktitut for "it is bright, it is lit" (The Art Newspaper).
- The main building by Gustavo da Roza opened on 25 September 1971, clad in Tyndall stone (Wikipedia).
- Qaumajuq, a four-storey building by Michael Maltzan with Cibinel Architecture, has an 8,000 square foot exhibition space, a 90-seat theatre and a glass vault holding around 5,000 Inuit works (The Art Newspaper).
- In June 2024 police confirmed that Astral Plain Scouts, donated in 2000 as a Norval Morrisseau painting, was a fake found in the Thunder Bay Morrisseau fraud investigation (CBC News, 26 June 2024).

FUNDING
Winnipeg Art Gallery-Qaumajuq: mixed public and private: governments and private donors built Qaumajuq, and Nunavut pays to keep its collection there
- Qaumajuq cost about 65 million dollars; about half came from all levels of government and the rest from private donors and businesses (CBC News, 11 March 2021).
- Under a 2025 agreement that stems from a 2015 Nunavut and Manitoba memorandum, the Government of Nunavut pays 715,000 dollars over five years for the gallery to keep more than 7,000 works from its collection until 2030 (Nunatsiaq News, 28 May 2025).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Two Art Newspaper reports raise the gallery's colonial collecting history and its former director Ferdinand Eckhardt's Nazi ties; neither addresses current funding.
1. The Art Newspaper: Gabriella Angeleti, "World's biggest Inuit art collection revealed at Winnipeg's new museum within a museum" (2021)
URL: https://www.theartnewspaper.com/news/winnipeg-gallery-opens-new-centre-dedicated-to-inuit-art
- Eckhardt, director from 1953, bought Inuit carvings from the Hudson's Bay Company and was later criticised for framing Inuit works as an advanced form of folk art.
- Some critics argued that later shows ignored the company's role in commercialising and exploiting Inuit artists; most of the Inuit collection came from Winnipeg collectors with ties to it.
2. The Art Newspaper: Elena Goukassian, "Canadian museum removing name of former director and Nazi supporter Ferdinand Eckhardt from its entrance hall" (2024)
URL: https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2024/01/11/winnipeg-art-gallery-removing-name-nazi-supporter-ferdinand-eckhardt
- Reports Conrad Sweatman's November 2023 investigation for The Walrus: Eckhardt signed an oath of allegiance to Hitler and later worked for IG Farben.
- On 19 December 2023 the gallery began removing his name and promised provenance research on Eckhardt donations; Borys had earlier told the Winnipeg Free Press there were "no gaps in the provenance".

BOTTOM LINE
Public and private money with no flagged funder on record. The weak point is the collection's history, and the Eckhardt provenance research is worth following.
