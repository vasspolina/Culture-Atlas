ANCHORAGE MUSEUM: Anchorage, United States
ID: anchorage-museum-anchorage
Website: https://www.anchoragemuseum.org (checked 2026-10-20)

OVERVIEW
- Art, history and science museum at 625 C Street in downtown Anchorage, Alaska, on the block the municipality named the Rasmuson Center in 1996 for Elmer and Mary Louise Rasmuson.
- Founded in 1967 as the Anchorage Historical and Fine Arts Museum with federal Alaska Purchase Centennial money, on land the city council reserved in 1966; it opened on 18 July 1968 and took its present name in 2009 (museum timeline).
- The municipality owns the building and collections; the private non-profit Anchorage Museum Association runs it under a long-term contract, and the Anchorage Museum Foundation holds the endowment (Wikipedia).
- A 26,000 sq ft glass-fronted wing by David Chipperfield Architects opened in 2009, and the Rasmuson Wing, by McCool Carlson Green, opened in 2017, bringing the building to 247,000 sq ft (museum timeline).
- Holds about 25,000 objects and 500,000 historic photographs; the Smithsonian Arctic Studies Center has had an office there since 1992 and galleries since 2010; the ConocoPhillips Gallery on the second floor shows contemporary Alaska Native art.
- Winter hours Tuesday to Saturday 10:00 to 18:00 and Sunday 12:00 to 18:00, closed Monday; summer daily 10:00 to 18:00.
- Adults $25, Alaska residents $20, seniors, military and students $23, children 6 to 12 $12; free for under-6s, members, enrolled tribal members, SNAP/EBT holders and on First Fridays after 18:00; $5 sensory-friendly Second Sundays for visitors with disabilities.

FUNDING
Anchorage Museum: mixed: city operating contract, foundations, admissions and corporate gifts
- In 2023 the municipal operating contract paid $5,529,908; foundations gave $1,021,475, corporations $352,088, federal government $238,097 and state and local government $13,000; admissions brought $1,678,862 (2023 Gratitude Report).
- Form 990 for 2023: revenue $12,653,212, contributions $9,938,592, expenses $12,631,751 (ProPublica).
- Elmer Rasmuson gave $50 million in 1999 to expand the museum, and the Rasmuson family paid for the 2017 wing (Anchorage Daily News).
- The 2022 contributors list, without amounts, names Chevron Corporation, ConocoPhillips Alaska Inc, Alyeska Pipeline Service Co, Bank of America, Bloomberg Philanthropies, the Rasmuson Foundation, the National Endowment for the Humanities and the Alaska State Council on the Arts.
- No later list naming corporate donors was found; the 2023 report gives category totals only.

Red flags: oil company donors and a ConocoPhillips-named gallery
- Chevron Corporation, ConocoPhillips Alaska and Alyeska Pipeline Service Co appear on the 2022 contributors list, the newest one that names donors.
- The contemporary Alaska Native art gallery carries the name ConocoPhillips Gallery on the museum's site in 2026; the page gives no sponsorship terms.
- No reporting found of a protest at the museum over these donors.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Little criticism of the institution; one local columnist attacked its earlier architecture and leadership, and art press coverage is mostly admiring.
1. Anchorage Daily News: Charles Wohlforth, opinion column on the museum under Julie Decker (2017)
URL: https://adn.com/opinions/2017/04/24/at-the-expanding-anchorage-museum-a-home-grown-leader-seeks-to-remake-alaskas-identity
- Calls the Chipperfield wing "trophy architecture without local context" and says earlier leadership "brought in commercial schlock for paying customers".
- Reports staff worries about a "largely unaccountable private museum board"; supports director Julie Decker.
2. The Art Newspaper: Gabriella Angeleti, on Nicholas Galanin's "Created to Hold Power" (2020)
URL: https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2020/05/08/nicholas-galanin-considers-the-unbalanced-power-of-institutions-over-indigenous-objects-in-anchorage-museum-exhibition
- Galanin's online show, hosted by the museum, attacks museum control of Indigenous objects.
- Does not name the Anchorage Museum as a target; it points at the Natural History Museum in New York and the Met.

BOTTOM LINE
The museum runs largely on city money and foundations, chiefly Rasmuson, with corporations giving a small share. The weak points are current oil donors (Chevron, ConocoPhillips, Alyeska) and a gallery of Alaska Native art named for ConocoPhillips. Corporate donor names after 2022 are not published.
