NATIONAL TAIWAN MUSEUM OF FINE ARTS: Taichung, Taiwan
ID: national-taiwan-museum-of-fine-arts-taichung
Website: https://www.ntmofa.gov.tw

OVERVIEW
- State art museum in Taichung, known as NTMoFA (Wikipedia).
- Opened on 26 June 1988 under the Taiwan Provincial Government's education department, originally as the Museum of Art; it closed in 1999 for earthquake-damage renovation and reopened in July 2004 (Wikipedia).
- Placed under the Council of Cultural Affairs, which Wikipedia says was restructured as the Ministry of Culture on 20 May 2012.
- The main building has four storeys, about 37,953 square metres of building area and a 102,000 square metre outdoor courtyard, with 16 galleries (Wikipedia).
- Outdoor features include a sculpture park of 45 sculptures and a forest of 50 calligraphy stone tablets (Wikipedia).
- It has organised Taiwan's pavilion at the Venice Architecture Biennale since 2000 and runs the International Biennial Print Exhibition and the Taiwan Biennial (Wikipedia).
- Closed Mondays; the main building opens Tuesday to Friday 12:00 to 18:00, Saturday 10:00 to 20:00 and Sunday 10:00 to 18:00; admission is free except for special exhibitions (Wikipedia).

FUNDING
National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts: mainly public: Taiwan's Ministry of Culture
- The museum is a Ministry of Culture institution (Wikipedia).
- The museum's donations page lists reports on donations received from outside bodies from 2016 to the January to June 2026 report, dated 25 August 2026 (museum website).

WIDER CONTEXT
- August 2026 naming dispute at the 16th Gwangju Biennale: a biennale poster listed Taiwan's satellite exhibition as the "NTMoFA Pavilion". Curator Chen Xiang-Wen and nine artists objected on 15 August, saying the name was changed without their consent and "brutally erased Taiwan from the poster" (The Art Newspaper, Lisa Movius, 26 August 2026).
- The Gwangju Biennale Foundation said a 12 January 2026 agreement set the museum-based name, because the pavilion was an institutional one. Taiwan's Ministry of Culture said it was agreed as the "Taiwan Pavilion" under an agreement signed on 2 April. On 25 August, after agreeing with the ministry, the foundation approved the name "Taiwan Pavilion" (The Art Newspaper).
- ArtReview (staff report, 26 August 2026) reports that the pavilion's artists and curators, the ministry and a letter signed by twenty other contributors called the change "political censorship". The organisers called the listing an "administrative convention" and said the National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts signed the agreement as an institutional participant, not a national one.
- The Art Newspaper reports that Chen suggested pressure from larger forces behind the scenes, with no evidence that the Chinese or South Korean governments were involved; the foundation said it did not intervene in or censor the exhibition's content. The objection was aimed at the Gwangju Biennale Foundation, not the museum.
- Central News Agency (udn, 24 August 2026) reported that 21 Gwangju art organisations signed a joint statement supporting Taiwan and aimed at the biennale organisation, whose decision-making it questioned.
- The connection to the museum: according to the artists' statement quoted by The Art Newspaper, the museum submitted the proposal under the Taiwan Pavilion name; the organisers say it signed the agreement as an institutional participant, and its acronym was used for the pavilion.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the museum is Taiwan's public budget through the Ministry of Culture, with outside donations reported in dated lists on its site. Nothing in the sources links a flagged funder to the museum. The 2026 Gwangju row was aimed at the biennale foundation's labelling of the pavilion; the museum was the named institutional participant.
