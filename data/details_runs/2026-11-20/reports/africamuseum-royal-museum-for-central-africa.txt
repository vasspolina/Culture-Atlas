AFRICAMUSEUM (ROYAL MUSEUM FOR CENTRAL AFRICA): Tervuren, Belgium
ID: africamuseum-royal-museum-for-central-africa
Website: https://www.africamuseum.be (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Federal museum and research institute in Tervuren, outside Brussels; it has some 190 employees, 50 of them scientists, and is led by director general Bart Ouvry (museum website).
- Began when the colonial section of the 1897 Brussels International Exposition moved to Tervuren at King Leopold II's request; it became the first permanent museum of the Congo in 1898, and the museum states that Leopold saw it as "a propaganda tool for his colonial project" (museum website).
- King Albert I inaugurated Charles Girault's palace on 30 April 1910 (museum website).
- Closed to the public on 1 December 2013 for a renovation by the Stéphane Beel Architecten consortium and reopened on 8 December 2018 with a new visitor centre; public space grew from 6,000 to 11,000 square metres (museum website).
- Collections include 10 million zoological specimens, 120,000 ethnographic objects and 8,000 musical instruments (museum website).
- In June 2021 Belgium transferred ownership of almost 800 looted objects to the Congolese state, and in February 2022 gave the DRC a list of more than 84,000 objects (Julien Bobineau, The Conversation, 2023).
- In February 2026 Belgian authorities refused KoBold Metals, a US mining company backed by Bill Gates, exclusive access to the colonial-era Congolese geological archives held at the museum, which said it cannot delegate their management to a private company (Brussels Times, 12 February 2026).
- Visitors: 106,000 in 2024, down from 132,000 in 2023; a spokesperson blamed tram and road works (Brussels Times, January 2025).

FUNDING
AfricaMuseum: mainly public: the Belgian federal state, with the National Lottery and corporate sponsors
- Operator: one of Belgium's ten federal scientific institutes, under the Secretary of State for Federal Science Policy; the Federal Science Policy Office (BELSPO) supervises it and chairs its Financial Management Committee (museum website).
- Buildings: the Belgian Buildings Agency owns and manages the buildings and park and carried out the renovation, the reception pavilion and the underground gallery (museum website), which cost over 65 million euros (Africa Is a Country, 2019).
- Development aid: the Directorate-General for Development Cooperation (DGD) is the main partner paying for its development cooperation work, and has backed its Prize Digital for Development since 2016 (museum website).
- National Lottery: a "key sponsor" of the renovation and of temporary and scientific exhibitions; the National Bank of Belgium gave money to the renovation (museum website).
- Corporate sponsors of the renovated museum: TEXAF, Puilaetco Dewaay, Ackermans & van Haaren and KBC; Umicore supports work on Central Africa's sustainable development and doctoral research by African students; Groupe Adecco is listed as a partner (museum website). No amounts are published.
- Private donors: over 100, including the Philippe Croonenberghs family and Christine Coene, widow of Luc Coene, who chaired the sponsoring committee (museum website).
- Founding money: the museum states that the original building works were paid for from the profits of the royal private domain of the Congo (museum website).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Criticism comes from historians and diaspora voices rather than the art press.
1. Africa Is a Country: Gillian Mathys, Margot Luyckfasseel, Sarah Van Beurden and Tracy Tansia, "Renovating the AfricaMuseum" (2019)
URL: https://africasacountry.com/2019/04/renovating-the-africamuseum
- Luyckfasseel rejects the museum's framing of itself as an impartial forum: "neutrality always favors the strong".
- Van Beurden says an nkisi nkondi is labelled "collected by Alex Delcommune" with no word on how it was obtained.
- Mathys notes that the colonial history room names no companies, though Umicore owes part of its success to exploitation by its predecessor Union Minière du Haut Katanga; Tansia says dismissing diaspora voices "when it does not suit sponsors, former colonial elites, or possible investors" is not decolonising.
2. Cultures of History Forum: Zofia Wóycicka, "Too Beautiful to Hurt Anyone: The New Permanent Exhibition at the Africa Museum Tervuren" (2020)
URL: https://cultures-of-history.uni-jena.de/exhibitions/too-beautiful-to-hurt-anyone-the-permanent-exhibition-at-the-africamuseum-tervuren
- Reports that director Guido Gryseels agrees with restitution claims in general but leaves the solution to the national government.
- Says colonial-era displays remain, including the "Crocodile room" and a statue of the Luba chief Lusinga taken as war booty.
- Reports that COMRAF, the diaspora advisory committee, said its members had a limited impact on the exhibition.

WIDER CONTEXT
- In its report on its February 2019 visit to Belgium (A/HRC/42/59/Add.1, 14 August 2019), the UN Working Group of Experts on People of African Descent judged that the steps taken to reorganise the museum "have not gone far enough" and noted "the importance of removing all colonial propaganda".
- Reporting the experts' view on 12 February 2019, the Brussels Times quoted then director Guido Gryseels saying the museum wanted "a new, more honest narrative".
- Activists defaced the bust of King Leopold II twice in 2020, after the killing of George Floyd and the Black Lives Matter protests (Zofia Wóycicka, Cultures of History Forum, 2020).
- Both concern the museum itself: the UN experts toured it with its director, and the bust stood there.

BOTTOM LINE
Mostly federal money with no flagged funder. The weak points are its colonial origins, the pace of restitution and undisclosed corporate sponsorship.
