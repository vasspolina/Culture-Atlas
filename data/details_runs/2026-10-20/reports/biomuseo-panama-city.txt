BIOMUSEO: Panama City, Panama
ID: biomuseo-panama-city
Website: https://biomuseo.org/en/ (checked 2026-10-20)

OVERVIEW
- Museum of Panama's natural and human history on the Amador Causeway at the south entrance of the Panama Canal (Wikipedia; stated by the space).
- Run by Fundación Amador, a non-profit set up in 2000; the executive director is Victor Cucalón Imbert and the board president is Liza Henríquez (stated by the space).
- Designed by Frank Gehry, his first building in Latin America; conceived in 1999, the first five galleries opened on 2 October 2014 and the last three in March 2019 (Wikipedia).
- A 4,000-square-metre building with eight galleries and an atrium facing the canal, in a free botanical garden; groundbreaking was in 2005 on a $60 million budget (The Tico Times, 2014).
- The eight permanent galleries, designed by Bruce Mau Design, run from the Gallery of Biodiversity to Panama is the Museum (Wikipedia).
- Visiting: open Tuesday to Sunday, 9:00 to 15:00 on weekdays and 10:00 to 15:00 at weekends. Adults pay B/.10 (residents) or B/.20 (non-residents); children 5 to 17, students and seniors pay less; under-5s free; 50% off with a SENADIS disability certificate. No accessibility details found on the site.

FUNDING
Biomuseo: mixed: state building money, then donations and ticket sales
- 2011: the cabinet approved an extra B/.8 million for construction, when the project cost was put at B/.90.3 million (Panamá América).
- 2016: the state agreed to give $18 million over three years for phase B (the aquarium, Living Web and Panama is the Museum galleries, a botanical park) and some running costs, through an agreement between the Ministry of Economy and Finance and Fundación Amador, from the 2017 budget (Panamá América).
- The state provided the land and owns the museum; the foundation board includes government members, among them the Comptroller General (Roberto Eisenmann, La Prensa opinion column, 2020).
- Private and citizen donations, some near B/.1 million each (Eisenmann, La Prensa).
- 2023: the museum lives "mainly from donations"; before the crisis the museum had reached a break-even point of about 85% of its income (foundation president Ana Lucrecia Tovar, La Prensa); a day open cost about $8,000 to $9,000 (La Prensa, November 2023).
- Corporate members: AES, CAU Internacional, Compañía Digital de Televisión, Escapes Magazine, Grupo Grada, Guidebooks, JIDSA, Kantar Mercaplan and Mapas de Panamá (stated by the space, no amounts or dates). Copa Airlines runs a ticket promotion; BAC Credomatic sponsored an evening event (stated by the space).
- The October 2023 blockades against the state's mining contract with Minera Panamá cut visits; the museum opened only at weekends, cut payroll by 50% and lost 70% of its cash flow (La Prensa). The protests were not aimed at the museum.

Red flags: fossil fuels (AES)
- AES is a corporate member of the museum (stated by the space).
- AES Panamá owns the AES Colón power station, at least 381 MW, which burns LNG and is listed with a 2018 start; the Costa Norte LNG Terminal imports gas for it. Global Energy Monitor lists AES Panamá's parents as the Government of Panama (50.4%) and AES Corp (49.1%).
- The membership page does not say which AES company is the member or what it pays.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Little criticism found; the one critical piece, in a newspaper rather than the art press, attacks the building's look.
1. The Tico Times: unsigned opening report, headed "Wondrous or hideous?" (2014)
URL: https://www.ticotimes.net/2014/10/03/wondrous-or-hideous-frank-gehry-designed-natural-history-museum-opens-to-fanfare-in-panama
- Calls the building a "spectacular monstrosity" with garish roofs that clash with the city's grey towers.
- Reports the project ran about $40 million over budget and funding problems repeatedly halted construction.
- Criticises the building, not the funding.

BOTTOM LINE
The building was paid for largely by the Panamanian state, and running costs now depend on donations and tickets, which leaves the museum exposed when visits fall. The one flagged funder is AES, a corporate member tied to an LNG power plant part-owned by the state. Worth watching: what AES pays and whether the foundation publishes accounts.
