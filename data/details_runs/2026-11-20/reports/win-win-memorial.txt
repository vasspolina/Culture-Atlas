WIN-WIN MEMORIAL: Phnom Penh, Cambodia
ID: win-win-memorial
Website: none (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Monument and museum on an 8 hectare plaza on the Chroy Changvar peninsula, near the Morodok Decho National Sports Complex (Phnom Penh Post, 2018).
- Honours 29 December 1998, which the government marks as the end of Cambodia's civil war, and Prime Minister Hun Sen's "win-win" policy of reintegrating Khmer Rouge soldiers (Phnom Penh Post, The Diplomat).
- Construction began on 25 February 2016 (Phnom Penh Post); a Defence Ministry spokesman, General Chhum Socheat, said its pillars would mark the successes of Hun Sen's policy (Cambodia Daily, 2016).
- Hun Sen inaugurated it in military uniform on 29 December 2018 (Phnom Penh Post, The Diplomat).
- Design: Modernist Khmer style; a pentagonal base 117 metres a side, which the Phnom Penh Post says represents Hun Sen's five-point "Divide, Isolate, Finish, Integrate and Develop" strategy, under a 33 metre triangular monolith for the three guarantees given to the Khmer Rouge. Built from 29,000 square metres of marble, 15,000 square metres of concrete and 3,000 tonnes of steel, with eight pools and bas-reliefs (Phnom Penh Post).
- The Diplomat reports that the base holds historical documents.
- Smaller copies followed: in May 2020 the government announced win-win memorials in several provinces, the first, 43 metres high, in Koh Kong; Nem Sowath of the Defence Ministry said the money "would not come from the government budget" (Cambodianess).

FUNDING
Win-Win Memorial: state and tycoon: Phnom Penh Municipal Hall and senator Ly Yong Phat
- Construction cost: $12 million, not counting the land, according to Hun Sen at the opening (Phnom Penh Post, 31 December 2018).
- Main payer: Phnom Penh Municipal Hall; Hun Sen said "most of the budget we have spent here is from Phnom Penh Municipal Hall" (Phnom Penh Post).
- Ly Yong Phat: a CPP senator and one of Cambodia's wealthiest businessmen, he donated the land (Cambodia Daily, 2016) and paid the rest of the construction cost; Hun Sen asked him to "continue to contribute as requested by the construction committee" (Phnom Penh Post).
- Public donations: in 2016 the Defence Ministry spokesman would not disclose the financial plan and urged Cambodians at home and abroad to donate, saying "the more contributions, the faster construction will be" (Cambodia Daily).
- Further spending: Tea Banh, Minister of National Defence, said three ministries supported construction, that a planned underground library, more statues and events were not in the $12 million, and that the construction committee had asked Hun Sen for more funds (Phnom Penh Post).

Red flags: US sanctions on land donor Ly Yong Phat over forced labour in scam centres, 2024
- On 12 September 2024 the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control sanctioned Ly Yong Phat, his L.Y.P. Group and O-Smach Resort "for their role in serious human rights abuse related to the treatment of trafficked workers subjected to forced labor in online scam centers", under Executive Order 13818, the Global Magnitsky sanctions programme (US Treasury press release).
- Treasury also designated Garden City Hotel, Koh Kong Resort and Phnom Penh Hotel as owned or controlled by him (US Treasury).
- RFA called him a casino tycoon known for controlling large parts of Cambodia's sugar industry for more than a decade (RFA, 12 September 2024).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: No art press coverage; the criticism comes from political journalism and treats the memorial as a monument by Hun Sen to himself.
1. The Diplomat: Andrew Nachemson, "Hun Sen's Monument to Himself" (2018)
URL: https://thediplomat.com/2018/12/hun-sens-monument-to-himself/
- Calls it a "self-congratulatory memorial" lined with carvings of Hun Sen's political triumphs, built from the same stone as Angkor Wat.
- Quotes Occidental College professor Ear Sophal: ending the war was "a genuine diplomatic achievement, but not nearly as impressive as he would have us believe".
- Quotes exiled opposition leader Sam Rainsy, an openly partisan source, calling it a "crazy monument" that distorts history and wastes money.
- Argues the carvings recast the dissolution of the opposition Cambodia National Rescue Party (CNRP) as a "win" for Cambodia.

BOTTOM LINE
Not clean: the private money behind it comes from a sanctioned tycoon, and the public money paid for a monument to the leader who opened it. Worth watching: who pays for the provincial copies.
