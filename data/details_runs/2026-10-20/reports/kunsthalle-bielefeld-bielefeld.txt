KUNSTHALLE BIELEFELD: Bielefeld, Germany
ID: kunsthalle-bielefeld-bielefeld
Website: https://www.kunsthalle-bielefeld.de/ (checked 2026-10-24)

OVERVIEW
- Art museum at Artur-Ladebeck-Straße 5 in Bielefeld, North Rhine-Westphalia, with a sculpture park beside the building.
- The collection began in 1950 with a donation from the businessman Rudolf August Oetker and grew through municipal purchases from 1954. The building opened with a "silent" ceremony on 27 September 1968, accompanied by protests (Wikipedia). Joachim Wolfgang von Moltke was the first director.
- The building was planned as the Richard-Kaselowsky-Haus, named for Oetker's stepfather, and the city dropped the name in 1998; the museum is now simply Kunsthalle Bielefeld.
- Designed by the US architect Philip Johnson, his only museum building in Europe. Red Main sandstone facade with large window fronts, three floors above ground and two below, about 1,200 square metres of exhibition space; it is a listed building.
- On site: exhibition floors, a café, an art education space, a film and lecture hall, a public library and the sculpture park.
- Visiting: closed Monday and Tuesday from October 2026, open Wednesday 11 to 21 and Thursday to Sunday 11 to 18. Adults pay 10 euros, reduced entry is 6 euros, and visitors under 18 enter free. A general renovation and expansion is planned from mid-2027.

FUNDING
Kunsthalle Bielefeld: mixed public and private: three shareholders, the city, a foundation and a savings bank, after a building gift from Oetker
- The museum names its supporters as the City of Bielefeld, the Kulturstiftung Kunsthalle Bielefeld and Sparkasse Bielefeld, and states it is a non-profit limited company (stated by the space; no years or amounts given).
- The 1968 building was financed largely by Rudolf August Oetker: his group paid 10.25 million Deutsche Mark of 12.5 million DM in costs, which came to about 4.6 million DM after tax deductions (German Wikipedia).
- In 2022 Bielefeld's city council approved a renovation budget of 40.5 million euros; Caruso St John was chosen as architect in 2024 (Wikipedia). The client is the city's property and service agency (Caruso St John).
- No reporting found of the amounts each shareholder pays toward running costs.
- No reporting found of Oetker family payments to the museum today. In 1998 Oetker ended his support and withdrew his loans after the council dropped the Kaselowsky name (Wikipedia; WSWS).

Red flags: Oetker family money and Nazi-era history
- Rudolf August Oetker volunteered for the Waffen-SS in July 1941 and became an SS-Untersturmführer der Reserve in October 1944 (BRF Nachrichten, dpa, 20 October 2013).
- The 2013 study "Dr. Oetker und der Nationalsozialismus" by Jürgen Finger, Sven Keller and Andreas Wirsching was published in October 2013; the group's chairman August Oetker said his father "was a National Socialist" (The Local, 17 October 2013).
- Forced labour: Wirsching said the group profited from the forced-labour system at companies where it held stakes, but not at the main Oetker plant. German Wikipedia names the Gundlach printing house and the Adler sewing-machine works, which had converted to armaments production.

Red flags: protest over the Kaselowsky name
- Kaselowsky (1888 to 1944) was an Oetker partner, NSDAP member and member of Himmler's circle of friends (Jochen Rath, Bielefeld City Archive, in the museum's journal).
- Opening ceremonies were cancelled in 1968 and a demonstration took place outside on 27 September 1968. Composer Hans Werner Henze withdrew a piano concerto written for the opening (Wikipedia).
- Protest returned in waves: students in 1971, a 1985 motion by the Greens/Bunte Liste to rename the house for Käthe Kollwitz, a residents' petition, and a 1998 council vote to drop the name (Irene Below, museum journal).
- A plaque listing Kaselowsky among "the victims of the Second World War" was replaced after a 2016 citizens' initiative; Rath dates the change to 2017, Below to 2018.
- Unverified: after the 1998 vote Oetker threatened to return his honorary citizenship and move the company to Hamburg, from the World Socialist Web Site, an openly Trotskyist outlet; the article notes he had not done so.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: No art-press piece on the museum's naming or funders was found. The criticism sits in the museum's own journal, in texts by historians for its 2023 symposium, and is listed here for that reason.
1. Kunsthalle Bielefeld journal: Jochen Rath, "Repeatedly finally 'discussed out'!? Biographical and Memory Cultural Observations on Richard Kaselowsky" (2023)
URL: https://kunsthalle-bielefeld.de/en/entdecken/kb-journal/repeatedly-finally-discussed-out-biographical-and-memory-cultural-observations-on-richard-kaselowsky/
- Describes the 1968 naming as opposed first by left-wing young people, then by national media and academics, while the city, the local press and the Oetker family defended it.
- Argues that the Oetker family's concession on the 2017 plaque ended the public debate and that chances to keep it going were missed.
- Published by the museum itself, so not independent art press.
2. Kunsthalle Bielefeld journal: Irene Below, "Art has WUMM" (2023)
URL: https://kunsthalle-bielefeld.de/en/blog/art-has-wumm-the-kunsthalle-and-its-naming-as-a-site-of-civil-society-debates-and-artistic-interventions/
- Argues the entrance plaque recast the company's Nazi-era role as a sacrifice for the city and omitted the victims of the Nazi regime.
- Asks who has a say in the building, collection and programme.
- Published by the museum itself.

WIDER CONTEXT
- The Oetker group's wartime record was examined only after the 2013 study. Wirsching said the results were neither a whitewash nor evidence of deep involvement in Nazi crimes (as relayed by BRF Nachrichten).
- The museum's own symposium page describes Philip Johnson as "an avowed National Socialist in the 1920s and 30s"; the sculpture garden was not named for him because of this (German Wikipedia).
- Connection: the Oetker gift built the museum and the family history is the subject of the naming dispute above.

BOTTOM LINE
The running costs come from the city, a foundation and a savings bank, none of which has a documented controversy in the pages read. The weak points are the building's origin in Oetker family money, the family's Nazi-era record including profit from forced labour at stakeholdings, and the open dispute over the Kaselowsky name. Watch the 40.5 million euro renovation and how the museum presents the history in it.
