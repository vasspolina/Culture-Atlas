HEROES' ACRE (WINDHOEK): Windhoek, Namibia
ID: heroes-acre-windhoek
Website: none

OVERVIEW
- National war memorial and burial ground in uninhabited hills 10 kilometres south of central Windhoek, on the B1 road to Rehoboth; it opened on 26 August 2002 (Wikipedia; New Era, 2017).
- Sam Nujoma conceived the idea in 1997 at an OAU summit in Harare (New Era, 2006). North Korea's Mansudae Overseas Projects designed and built it, and Van der Hoog calls it, like Zimbabwe's Acre, a copy of the Revolutionary Martyrs' Cemetery near Pyongyang (Leiden University thesis, 2024).
- The City of Windhoek donated the land (New Era, 2017); the tourism site Navigate Namibia says Mansudae built the site in 13 months.
- A marble obelisk and a bronze Unknown Soldier at the centre, a grandstand for 5,000 and 174 graves (New Era, 2017; Wikipedia).
- Presidents Hage Geingob (state funeral in February 2024, the first burial in the presidential mausoleums) and Sam Nujoma (state funeral on 1 March 2025) lie there (New Era, 2025; Wikipedia).
- Visitors: 7,533 from April 2005 to March 2006 against an expected 20,000 (New Era, 2006); in 2017 most daily visitors were international tourists (New Era, 2017).
- Visiting: for 1 April 2024 to 30 April 2025 admission was N$50 for Namibian adults self-guided and N$60 guided, N$70 and N$80 for international adults, with children up to 7 free (Government Gazette No. 8206).

FUNDING
Heroes' Acre (Windhoek): public: Government of Namibia paid for the build; the National Heritage Council runs it
- The Acre was budgeted at N$34 million and built for N$60 million (Van der Hoog, p.147; New Era, 2006).
- Running it: in 2006 admission fees raised N$64,188 against upkeep of N$500,000 a year, described as a drain on the National Monuments Council's meagre finances (Van der Hoog, p.147).
- The National Heritage Council, the statutory body under the National Heritage Act 2004, runs the site (New Era, 2017) and sets the fees with the minister's approval (Government Gazette No. 8206, 4 September 2023). The Ministry of Works and Transport has the mandate of keeping it up to standard (New Era, 2017); in 2006 its under secretary said three years without maintenance was too long (New Era, 2006).
- State funerals: 16 state funerals in 2024/25, including those of Geingob and Nujoma at Heroes' Acre, were part of N$38.4 million spent on state and official funerals, according to Prime Minister Elijah Ngurare (New Era, 28 April 2025).

Not related: the National Heroes' Acre near Harare, Zimbabwe, a separate site built by the same contractor and inaugurated in 1982 (Van der Hoog).

WIDER CONTEXT
- Contracting, 2002: Insight Namibia's Corruption Tracker (March 2008) said Mansudae's Heroes' Acre contract ended up costing twice as much as first expected, and that its State House contract went out with no competitive tender. Van der Hoog says African governments usually award Mansudae projects by closed tender, so total payments cannot be determined (p.148).
- Sanctions on the builder, 2016 to 2017: OFAC blocked Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies on 2 December 2016 (Federal Register). The UN Security Council listed it in Resolution 2371 of 5 August 2017, and the EU followed, naming Namibia among its countries of business (EU Regulation 2017/1457).
- The UN Panel of Experts (S/2017/150) lists the Hero's Acre among Mansudae's works in Namibia (table 4) and recommended its designation for arms-related services, building a munitions factory on behalf of KOMID and bulk cash transfers by its workers (para 118); the factory was at Oamites (para 110).
- Namibia announced in June 2016 that it would end the services of KOMID and Mansudae (S/2017/150, para 112). On 17 November 2017 it told the Panel that all North Korean workers had left and supplied cancellation orders for Mansudae projects (S/2018/171, paras 104 to 106). Van der Hoog notes the 2006 sanctions regime did not criminalise construction work (p.148).
- Mansudae built the Acre in 2002 as a contractor; it did not fund it.

BOTTOM LINE
Namibian public money built and runs the Acre, and no funder belongs to a flagged industry or a sanctions list. The contracting record and the builder's later sanctions are older context, not a current red flag.
