BUKU-LARRNGGAY MULKA CENTRE: Yirrkala, Australia
ID: buku-larrnggay-mulka-centre-yirrkala
Website: https://yirrkala.com (checked 2026-10-24)

OVERVIEW
- Indigenous community-controlled art centre in Yirrkala, Northern Territory, about 700 km east of Darwin, serving Yirrkala and about 25 homeland centres within 200 km (stated by the centre).
- Founded in 1976 when Yolŋu artists set up Buku-Larrŋgay Arts in the old Mission health centre, as the Methodist Overseas Mission withdrew; the centre was formerly called Buku-Larrŋgay Arts (Wikipedia). The beachfront gallery that Narritjin Maymuru opened in the 1960s is counted among its inspirations.
- The Mulka Project, a digital archive and production studio, was added in 2007 (stated by the centre).
- Building: a museum built with a Bicentennary grant in 1988; a screen print workshop and extra gallery space in 1996; a new air-conditioned workshop and covered outdoor area in September 2019 (Wikipedia).
- On site: the Yirrkala Art Centre, which sells work by Yolŋu artists (bark paintings, weaving, larrakitj memorial poles, yidaki), the Mulka Museum, the Mulka Archive and Mulka Production, and the Yirrkala Print Space. Holdings include the 1963 Yirrkala Church Panels. Around 20 staff, mainly Yolŋu (stated by the centre).
- Visiting: open Monday to Friday 8am to 4:30pm and Saturday 9am to 12pm. The centre's visit page says permits are required to be in the region. No admission fee or accessibility information is published.

FUNDING
Buku-Larrnggay Mulka Centre: mixed: federal arts support, self-generated sales, and grants from the Ian Potter Foundation and the Cotton On Foundation
- Core operational support from the Australian Government's Indigenous Visual Arts Industry Support (IVAIS) programme since 1998-99 (indigenous.gov.au; Wikipedia says it still received it as of 2024).
- Self-generated income: coordinator Will Stubbs said "The majority of our income is self-generated" (indigenous.gov.au).
- The Ian Potter Foundation: $300,000 over three years to Buku Larrŋgay Mulka Incorporated for "The Mulka Project: Cultural Careers in Digital Media Creation" (Ian Potter Foundation grants round-up, December 2024; round 3 of 2024).
- Cotton On Foundation, the charity arm of the Cotton On Group retailer: has worked with Yirrkala since 2013. It says it and its supporters have raised over AUD 1.2 million for educational projects in Yirrkala; its page also describes the Mulka Project's youth programme, and gives no separate Mulka figure (Cotton On Group).
- Rirratjingu Aboriginal Corporation, a Yolŋu landowner corporation set up in 1984 to use bauxite royalties for social and cultural programmes, contributes to the centre; its new performing arts stage is named for Roy Marika. No amount is given in the source (ORIC, 2017).
- The Northern Territory Government and the Indigenous Languages and Arts programme appear among the sponsor logos on the centre's home page, read from the logo file names (the logos carry no text). No amounts were confirmed from an opened source.
- No reporting found of a published board list or annual accounts on the centre's own site. The centre names Binygurr Wirrpanda as Chair.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: No criticism of the centre as an institution found in art media. Coverage of its exhibitions is admiring; the one review read below criticises catalogue and selection choices only.
1. Artist Profile: Ian McLean, review of "Yolŋu Power: The Art of Yirrkala" (2025)
URL: https://artistprofile.com.au/review-yolngu-power-the-art-of-yirrkala/
- Presents the Art Gallery of New South Wales show as an eighty-year history of sixteen clans drawn from the centre, and reads it as political: clans framed as independent nations in a Yolŋu federation.
- Finds the star artists Gunybi Ganambarr and Nonggirrnga Marawili "well if not over-represented" and calls the Madayin catalogue "far superior" to the Yolŋu Power one.
- Criticises the exhibition and catalogue, not the centre's money or governance.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the centre is public arts support, its own art sales, a charitable trust and a retail foundation, with Yolŋu landowner royalties contributing; no funder with a documented controversy was found. The weak point is transparency: the centre's own site lists no accounts, and several funding amounts could not be confirmed from opened sources. Watch the Territory and federal grant figures.
