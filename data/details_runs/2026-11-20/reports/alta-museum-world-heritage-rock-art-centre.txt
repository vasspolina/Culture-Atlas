WORLD HERITAGE ROCK ART CENTRE, ALTA MUSEUM: Alta, Norway
ID: alta-museum-world-heritage-rock-art-centre
Website: https://www.altamuseum.no (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Museum and world heritage centre at Hjemmeluft, about 5 km west of central Alta in Finnmark, beside one of the five rock art areas inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1985 (Alta Museum).
- Founded in 1978 as a municipal museum, it moved into a new building at Hjemmeluft in 1991 and became an intermunicipal company in 1997 (Store norske leksikon); the first rock carvings at Hjemmeluft were found in 1973 (Store norske leksikon).
- The museum building went up in two stages, finished in 1991 and 2013; most of the buildings the museum uses belong to Alta Municipality (economic plan 2026 to 2029).
- It manages the Rock Art of Alta world heritage site under an agreement with Finnmark County and does delegated work for the Struve Geodetic Arc world heritage site; its local history collection holds 3,440 objects and 6,500 photographs, plus nine historic buildings and fourteen boats, among them the protected vessel MK Vally from 1917 (annual report 2024).
- It won the European Museum of the Year Award in May 1993, the first Norwegian museum to do so (Alta Museum).
- It had 71,379 visitors in 2024, 57,468 of them paying, its second-highest count after the Olympic year 1994; growing cruise traffic drove much of the rise (annual report 2024).
- Director Jan S. Dølør; the board is chaired by Anita Persen (annual report 2024).
- Visiting: open all year; 2026 summer tickets NOK 175 for adults, NOK 165 for seniors, NOK 155 for students, NOK 50 for children 7 to 16, children under 7 free, with lower winter prices; the carvings can be seen only in the snow-free season; audio guides in six languages; the footpath between the rock art areas closed from 19 August (Alta Museum, annual report 2024).

FUNDING
Alta Museum: mainly public: Alta Municipality, Finnmark County and the Norwegian state, plus ticket, shop and café sales
- Owned by Alta Municipality and Finnmark County, each holding a 50 percent share (Alta Museum).
- Operating grants come from Alta Municipality, Finnmark County, the Ministry of Culture and the Norwegian Environment Agency, with project money from Riksantikvaren, the Environment Agency, the Directorate for Culture and others (annual report 2024).
- The 2026 budget sets operating grants of NOK 3,249,539 from Alta Municipality, NOK 1,754,090 from Finnmark County, NOK 4,685,000 from the Ministry of Culture and NOK 2,000,000 from the Environment Agency for its role as an authorised world heritage visitor centre, plus NOK 4,395,000 in Riksantikvaren project grants paid through the county; sales are budgeted at NOK 13.9M of NOK 33.7M total income (economic plan 2026 to 2029).
- Sales made up 39 percent of income in 2024, against a 27 percent average for Norwegian museums funded by the Ministry of Culture (annual report 2024).
- The museum says its state grant is among the lowest of the 60 museums funded by the Ministry of Culture and that it has asked for more since 2024; a 25 percent cut to its world heritage allocation in 2025 cost it about NOK 1M (economic plan 2026 to 2029).
- In 2021 it bought the property Moreneveien 25 to protect the Storsteinen world heritage locality, with support from Riksantikvaren and the then Troms and Finnmark County; rent from the property pays the loan (economic plan 2026 to 2029).
- Accounts for 2025: operating income NOK 35.3M, of which NOK 13.9M sales; surplus NOK 203,118 (Regnskapsregisteret).

BOTTOM LINE
Clean: public money from its two owners and the state, with a large share earned from visitors.
