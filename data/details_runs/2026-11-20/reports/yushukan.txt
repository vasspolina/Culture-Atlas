YŪSHŪKAN: Tokyo, Japan
ID: yushukan
Website: https://www.yasukuni.or.jp/english/yushukan/ (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- War museum in the grounds of Yasukuni Shrine; its name comes from a passage on learning in the Chinese classic Xunzi (museum website).
- Proposed at the end of the 1877 Seinan War and planned from 1879 under Army Minister Yamagata Aritomo, it opened on 25 February 1882 in a building in the style of an Italian medieval castle by the architect Giovanni Cappeletti (museum website).
- Before the war the Army Ministry handled Yasukuni's construction and accounting, so the Yushukan worked as a state military museum (Tsujita, Gendai Business, 2026).
- After the 1923 earthquake the present main building was built in the "Imperial crown" style, with Ito Chuta of Tokyo Imperial University as architectural adviser, and opened on 26 April 1932 (museum website).
- Its time as a museum ended on 11 September 1945, when the Emperor Meiji's order to preserve military artefacts was repealed (museum website).
- It reopened in July 1986; on 13 July 2002 the main building was renovated for the shrine's 130th anniversary, with a new building, a cinema and a glass hall for the Zero fighter (museum website).
- About 11,214 square metres of floor space; renovated again in 2025 for the 80th anniversary of the war's end (Tsujita, 2026).
- Visiting: daily 9.00 to 16.30, last entry 30 minutes before closing, with a few days closed at the end of June and December; 22 exhibit rooms; adults 1,000 yen, university students 500, secondary students 300, younger children free; wheelchairs, lifts and accessible toilets; no photography in the exhibit rooms (museum website).

FUNDING
Yushukan: private: Yasukuni Shrine, a religious corporation, and admission fees
- Owner and operator: Yasukuni Shrine, which became a religious corporation under Occupation orders after the war and took over the Yushukan (Tsujita, 2026).
- Rent: from November 1947 the shrine leased the building to Fukoku Mutual Life Insurance, which used it as its head office until 1980 (Wikipedia; museum website).
- Admission: entry is free for members of the Yasukuni Jinja Parishioners' Association and the Yushukan Museum Association, and discounted after formal worship at the shrine's main sanctuary (museum website).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Critics in Japan and abroad fault its account of the war; the museum rewrote some panels in 2006 and 2007 after criticism from US officials.
1. Reuters (NBC News): report on the shrine's plan to revise its exhibits (2006)
URL: https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna16289597
- Citing the Mainichi Shimbun, reported that the shrine had agreed in October 2006 to change text saying the United States deliberately forced Japan into the war, and would soften wording on China, including an account of the 1937 Marco Polo Bridge incident that blamed Chinese nationalist troops.
- An unnamed source involved said: "There is no mistake in the facts."
2. Gendai Business (Kodansha): Tsujita Masanori, "靖国史観は日本「全肯定」か...国際情勢とともに変わる靖国神社の歴史展示" (2026)
URL: https://gendai.media/articles/-/169604
- US Ambassador Schieffer and former Deputy Secretary of State Armitage criticised the panel "Roosevelt's grand strategy" (translated from Japanese) in 2006 for suggesting the US pushed Japan into war through an oil embargo; conservative commentator Okazaki Hisahiko also urged a change.
- The museum closed at the end of 2006 and reopened in January 2007 with the panel retitled and rewritten, which Tsujita finds more restrained.
- He argues the narrative remains a "passive view of history" (受け身史観), with Japan reacting rather than acting, and that the panels still neither explain why the US tightened sanctions nor address problems in Japan's own military actions.

WIDER CONTEXT
- Yasukuni Shrine is dedicated to soldiers who died fighting for the Emperor, including convicted war criminals (Wikipedia).
- The shrine owns and runs the museum, so disputes over the shrine attach to it.

BOTTOM LINE
Clean money: private religious funds and ticket sales. The weak point is its account of Japan's wars, softened in places after outside criticism and still disputed.
