OVERVIEW
- Private foundation showing the Julia Stoschek Collection of time-based media art, with a Berlin venue at Leipziger Strasse 60 and its headquarters and second venue in Düsseldorf (Berliner Zeitung, 25 April 2026; ArtReview, 2026).
- Julia Stoschek, born 1975, began buying art in 2003; she opened the Düsseldorf venue, a converted industrial building from 1907 with a cinema and an archive, in 2007, and the Berlin venue in June 2016 (English Wikipedia; Apollo, 2016).
- The collection holds more than 1,000 works of time-based art: video, film, performance, installation and virtual reality (ArtReview; USA Art News, 24 April 2026).
- The Berlin venue is the former Czech Cultural Centre, a GDR prefabricated building of 3,000 square metres owned by the Bundesanstalt für Immobilienaufgaben; it hosted 22 exhibitions, including Arthur Jafa, Meriem Bennani and Mark Leckey, and drew 450,000 visitors (Berliner Zeitung; ArtReview).
- In April 2026 the foundation announced it would close the Berlin venue at the end of October 2026 as a "strategic realignment" towards international projects; the Düsseldorf venue is being renovated and reopens in spring 2027 (Tagesspiegel, 24 April 2026; Berliner Zeitung; ArtReview).
- Its first major US show, "What a Wonderful World: An Audiovisual Poem", curated by Udo Kittelmann at the Variety Arts Theater in Los Angeles, drew more than 30,000 visitors (USA Art News; Berliner Zeitung).

FUNDING
Julia Stoschek Foundation: private: the money of collector Julia Stoschek, a shareholder in the Brose auto-parts group
- Stoschek is "an active shareholder" in Brose, the automotive-parts group founded by her great-grandfather; her father Michael Stoschek is its chairman (W Magazine, Diane Solway, 20 November 2017). Tagesspiegel and Berliner Zeitung (2026) also describe her as a Gesellschafterin (shareholder) of Brose.
- Brose reported sales of 7.7 billion euros for 2024 (English Wikipedia).
- Tagesspiegel (2026) writes that her Berlin programme of workshops, talks, loans to institutions and conservation of fragile early video consumes a high annual budget. No source opened says how the foundation is financed in detail.
- Six years before the closure the federal landlord demanded a drastic rent rise; Stoschek complained of a lack of support from the city and federal government, and the conflict was settled for a time with the help of culture senator Klaus Lederer (Berliner Zeitung, 25 April 2026).
- The collection co-sponsored two German Pavilion shows at the Venice Biennale, in 2015 and 2017 (English Wikipedia; W Magazine).

WIDER CONTEXT
- Brose was founded in Coburg on 14 June 1919 by Max Brose and Ernst Jühling. Max Brose joined the Nazi Party in 1933; in the Second World War the company made gasoline canisters and armaments for the military and used forced labour, including prisoners of war (English Wikipedia).
- The company and family say Max Brose treated workers well; English Wikipedia states that this claim "has not been substantiated".
- Tagesspiegel, Nicola Kuhn, "Die Sammlerin Julia Stoschek und ihr Urgroßvater: Schmutzige Gelder, saubere Kunst" (11 November 2020; paywalled, only the opening was read): during Art Week the artist Leon Kahane showed the installation "Jerrycans to can Jerry" near the collection on Leipziger Strasse, pointing to Brose's ties to the armaments industry under National Socialism.
- Berliner Zeitung, Hanno Hauenstein, "Ende der Documenta 15" (25 September 2022; opinion-led commentary): says Stoschek shields her great-grandfather's Nazi past "aktiv gegen Kritik" (actively against criticism), and that a Brose-commissioned history drew criticism for missing footnotes.
- Unverified: the same Berliner Zeitung piece says Michael Stoschek pressed in 2015 for a street to be renamed Max-Brose-Strasse and compared Brose to Oskar Schindler; that the artist Hito Steyerl bought back her work from the collection shortly after a text by David de Jong criticised rich families' handling of their Nazi past; and that the paper received an Abmahnung (warning letter) over a phrase in its translation of that text.
- The foundation's money comes from a Brose shareholder, which is the connection to this history.

BOTTOM LINE
The money is one heir's private fortune from a car-parts group, with no public or corporate sponsor named. The weak point is the Nazi-era forced labour behind that fortune and the family's handling of it. Watch what replaces the Berlin venue after October 2026.
