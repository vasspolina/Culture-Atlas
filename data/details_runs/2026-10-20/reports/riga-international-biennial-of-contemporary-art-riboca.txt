RIGA INTERNATIONAL BIENNIAL OF CONTEMPORARY ART (RIBOCA): Riga, Latvia
ID: riga-international-biennial-of-contemporary-art-riboca
Website: https://riboca.com (checked 2026-10-24; parked, redirects to a GoDaddy for-sale page)

OVERVIEW
- Contemporary art biennial in Riga, Latvia, focused on the Baltic region and its international connections; it has no permanent building and used sites around the city and in Jūrmala.
- Founded and directed by Agniya Mirgorodskaya through the Riga Biennial Foundation, announced in 2017; the first edition ran from 2 June to 28 October 2018 (Wikipedia).
- RIBOCA1, "Everything Was Forever, Until It Was No More", was curated by Katerina Gregos with an all-women team; it used eight sites, among them the former biology faculty, the Boļševička factory and the Andrejsala docks (The Art Newspaper, Wikipedia).
- RIBOCA2 ran from 20 August to 13 September 2020, curated by Rebecca Lamarche-Vadel, reduced by the pandemic to one exhibition at Andrejsala and a feature film (Wikipedia).
- RIBOCA3, "Exercises in Respect", curated by René Block, was postponed in 2022 after Russia's invasion of Ukraine; the 2023 programme, due to open 10 August, was suspended in July 2023 (ArtReview, Euronews).
- Visiting: no opening days or admission are current; no edition has been staged since 2020 and the website is parked.

FUNDING
RIBOCA: private: the founder's family money; the organisers said funding moved to a US charity after 2022
- 2018: the first edition "is funded by the St Petersburg-based fishing entrepreneur Gennady Mirgorodsky", described as half-owner of the Arkhangelsk trawler fleet and father of the founder (The Art Newspaper, 2018).
- Euronews (2023) states that Mirgorodskaya had accepted philanthropic support from her father.
- After the war began, financing was restructured to come through Just A Moment, a US charity registered by Mirgorodskaya and her husband Robert William Pokora, an American real estate investor (USA Art News).
- Pokora agreed to give a fixed percentage of his earnings to a new endowment fund; USA Art News calls this a temporary solution and says some critics were not satisfied.
- Art Focus Now (2023) states that only 150,000 euros came directly from Pokora's business and that the largest donation came from Mirgorodskaya herself; the page prints the amount as "around seven hundred Euros", which it does not explain.
- Art Focus Now also states that Pyotr Aven, co-owner of Alfa Bank, supported RIBOCA, with no amount given, and that applications for state funding went to the Latvian Ministry of Culture and the State Fund for Cultural Capital.
- No reporting found of an annual report, accounts, donor list or board published by RIBOCA.

Red flags: boycott and campaign against the biennial
- 30 June 2023: four Latvian artists withdrew from the third edition: Ēriks Božis, Maija Kurševa, Armands Zelčs and Amanda Ziemele (Art Focus Now).
- Kurševa said it was "impossible to accept money from a Russian business to produce commissioned artwork" (Art Focus Now).
- Curator Žanete Liekīte campaigned against the biennial and said the issue was ethical funding and transparency, not ethnicity (USA Art News).
- July 2023: the organisers suspended RIBOCA3, citing the heritage of their executive members as "too significant to overcome" (Biennial Foundation, ArtReview).

Red flags: Russian family money and a sanctioned board figure
- The founder's father Gennady Mirgorodsky, a Russian fishing businessman, financed the first edition (The Art Newspaper).
- Pyotr Aven, described by USA Art News as under EU sanctions, is named by Art Focus Now as a supporter; Liekīte said he left the RIBOCA board without any public mention (USA Art News).
- Inese Dabola, the executive director, is linked to a Latvian foundation of Aven (USA Art News).
- Unverified: that the Common Ground refugee initiative was partly a way to deflect questions about funding, from Art Focus Now, which reports it as a suspicion.

Not related: no similarly named entity found.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: The criticism is about the founders and the money, not the art. It comes mostly from Latvian curators and artists quoted in news coverage; no signed art-press essay was found.
1. USA Art News: Romario, "'What We Are Providing May Simply Be Unwanted'" (2023)
URL: https://usaartnews.com/news/what-we-are-providing-may-simply-be-unwanted-after-backlash-from-the-latvian-art-scene-for-its-links-to-russia-the-riga-biennial-has-called-off-its-third-edition
- Liekīte, in a Latvian opinion piece, argued that helping Ukrainian refugees without naming the aggressor is contradictory: "one hand reaching out to Ukraine, while the other is held on Russia's knee".
- Kurševa called the organisers' explanation about artist fees coming from other countries "a legal argument but entirely unpersuasive".
- Names Mirgorodskaya, Pokora and Aven; the page cites Artnet News for some quotes.
2. USA Art News: Romario, "Riga biennial cancelled after outcry over organisers' Russian links" (2023)
URL: https://usaartnews.com/news/riga-biennial-cancelled-after-outcry-over-organisers-russian-links
- Liekīte compared the case to Sackler family funding in New York and to Warren Kanders's funding of art while he ran a tear gas business.
- Curator Maija Rudovska also described Latvian art's reliance on Russian-linked money.
- Mirgorodskaya answered that she is as much Lithuanian as Russian and had lived outside Russia for about 15 years.
3. Art Focus Now: Grigory Nekhoroshev, "RIBOCA – Postponement or End?" (2023)
URL: https://artfocusnow.com/news/riboca-postponement-or-end/
- Critics saw Russian wealth as soft power and as unfair competition for state funds.
- Credits the biennial with raising Riga's international profile and supporting Latvian artists.
- Concludes that the biennial's Russian ties made it unwelcome in the current climate.

BOTTOM LINE
The biennial's money came from a Russian fishing businessman's family and, after 2022, from a US charity the founder and her husband registered; the organisers published no accounts or donor list, so the split cannot be checked. The weak points are the founder's family money, Aven's reported link and the lack of disclosure. Watch whether the biennial returns and, if it does, who pays.
