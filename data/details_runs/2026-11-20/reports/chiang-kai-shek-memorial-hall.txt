CHIANG KAI-SHEK MEMORIAL HALL: Taipei, Taiwan
ID: chiang-kai-shek-memorial-hall
Website: https://www.cksmh.gov.tw (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Memorial hall and park of about 25 hectares at Zhongshan South Road 21 in Taipei's Zhongzheng District; the park also holds the National Concert Hall and the National Theater (cksmh.gov.tw).
- After Chiang Kai-shek died on 5 April 1975, the Executive Yuan set up a group to build the hall on a site first planned as a commercial centre; it opened on 4 April 1980 (cksmh.gov.tw).
- The site was a Qing army camp, then the barracks of the Japanese 1st Taiwan Infantry Regiment, then the ROC Army headquarters after 1949 (cksmh.gov.tw).
- The architect was Yang Cho-cheng (romanisation by the atlas); the main hall is about 70 metres high and the front gate 30 metres high and 80 metres wide (cksmh.gov.tw).
- The hall's own history says it was modelled on ancient Chinese imperial palaces and became a place for reshaping the cult of authority, with memorial rallies and exhibitions honouring Chiang (cksmh.gov.tw).
- It houses a 6.3 metre bronze statue of Chiang (Al Jazeera, 2024).
- In 2007 it was renamed National Taiwan Democracy Memorial Hall and the gate inscription changed to "Liberty Square"; after the 2008 change of government all but the gate inscription were reversed, and the old name went back up in July 2009 (cksmh.gov.tw).
- In 2007 the culture ministry's predecessor listed the park as a national monument, and in 2008 Taipei registered it as a cultural landscape (cksmh.gov.tw).
- Since 2017 the Ministry of Culture has run a transformation programme; current displays include "Soul of Freedom vs. Dictator" (2022), which drew more than 350,000 visitors in three years, and the permanent exhibition "Flowers of Freedom", opened on 24 November 2025 (cksmh.gov.tw; titles translated by the atlas).
- The hall recorded 4,350,357 visits in 2025 (cksmh.gov.tw statistics).
- Chang Hui-chun (romanisation by the atlas), formerly head of the ministry's Taipei Cultural Center in New York, has directed it since 1 August 2025 (cksmh.gov.tw).
- Visiting: open daily 9:00 to 18:00, closed on days such as Lunar New Year's Eve and 28 February. An honour guard drill runs every hour from 9:00 to 17:00 on Democracy Boulevard (cksmh.gov.tw).

FUNDING
Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall: public: Taiwan central government
- The National Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall Management Office was run by the Taipei City education bureau, moved to the Ministry of Education in July 1986 and to the Ministry of Culture in May 2012 (cksmh.gov.tw).
- The 2025 central budget gave the office NT$203,339,000 under its "arts promotion and guidance" line (Newtalk, 2025).
- The 1980 opening ceremony described the hall as erected by compatriots at home and abroad in memory of Chiang (cksmh.gov.tw; translated by the atlas).

Red flags: Dispute over the statue and the 2024 removal of the honour guard
- From 15 July 2024 the defence ministry withdrew the honour guard's sentry duty from the statue hall and moved its drill outside, which the Ministry of Culture said was meant to end the "worshipping of authoritarianism" (cksmh.gov.tw; Taipei Times, 2024).
- Families of victims of the 1947 massacre by Chiang's troops have long demanded that the statue be removed (Al Jazeera, 2024).
- The hall lists a civic action titled "Taiwan does not need a dictator's memorial hall" among the protests held on its square (cksmh.gov.tw; translated by the atlas).
- In June 2026 Premier Cho Jung-tai (romanisation by the atlas) asked the Ministry of Culture for a spatial plan by the end of 2026 to turn the hall into a "democracy education park" (PTS, 2026).

Red flags: 2025 Kuomintang bid to cut the budget and halt transitional justice work
- During the January 2025 budget vote the Kuomintang caucus moved to cut NT$30 million from the hall's arts line and demanded that the ministry stop transitional justice work there, remove the "Soul of Freedom vs. Dictator" exhibition and return the honour guard to the statue hall (Newtalk).
- The Ministry of Culture called the demands "reversing democracy" (PTS, 2025; translated by the atlas).

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the hall is Taiwanese central government money. Its weak points are political: the main parties dispute what the state should commemorate there, and the planned conversion is worth watching.
