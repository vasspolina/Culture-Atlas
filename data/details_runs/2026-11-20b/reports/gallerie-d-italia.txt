GALLERIE D'ITALIA: Milan, Italy
ID: gallerie-d-italia
Website: https://www.gallerieditalia.com (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Museum network owned by the bank Intesa Sanpaolo, with sites in Milan, Naples, Turin and Vicenza (Italian Wikipedia).
- Vicenza, in Palazzo Leoni Montanari, opened in 1999 with 18th-century Venetian art; Naples followed in 2007 and Milan, on Piazza della Scala, on 3 November 2011 (Finestre sull'Arte; Italian Wikipedia).
- Turin, on Piazza San Carlo, opened on 17 May 2022 for photography and holds the bank's Publifoto archive; Naples moved from Palazzo Zevallos to the Palazzo del Banco di Napoli on via Toledo on 21 May 2022, fitted out by Michele De Lucchi (Italian Wikipedia).
- They show part of the bank's collection of more than 35,000 works, 3,500 of them of particular note (Finestre sull'Arte, 2024).
- Milan joins Palazzo Brentani, Palazzo Anguissola Antona Traversi and the former Banca Commerciale Italiana headquarters, with a 19th-century route, the "Cantiere del '900" and an open vault (museum website); Naples shows Caravaggio's "Martirio di sant'Orsola" (Italian Wikipedia).
- Michele Coppola, the bank's executive director for art, culture and historic assets, is general director of the museums (Finestre sull'Arte; Italian Wikipedia).
- Milan opens Tuesday to Sunday, 9:30 to 19:00. Tickets cost 6 euros, 3 euros reduced, and are free for under-26s, school groups, Intesa Sanpaolo customers, and a disabled visitor with one companion; it arranges visits for people with motor, cognitive and sensory disabilities (museum website, archived November 2026).

FUNDING
Gallerie d'Italia: corporate-owned: Intesa Sanpaolo
- Owner and operator: Intesa Sanpaolo, under its Progetto Cultura, a multi-year plan conceived by its president emeritus Giovanni Bazoli; it sits inside a programme that commits 1.5 billion euros to projects in Italy by 2027 (Finestre sull'Arte, 2024).
- Partner: Fondazione Cariplo, whose collection is shown in Milan (Italian Wikipedia).
- Visitors: 750,000 across the four sites in 2024, including 100,000 school students who take part free; Progetto Cultura produced twelve major shows and sixty side talks that year (Finestre sull'Arte).
- Buildings: the museums were made from historic palaces the bank owned and once used as offices, converted with major architectural restoration (Finestre sull'Arte).

Red flags: 2025 Extinction Rebellion occupation in Turin
- On 2 March 2025, the last day of the "American Nature" show, about 30 Extinction Rebellion activists entered with tickets and lay down in the room on fossil fuel damage, protesting the "investimenti dannosi" (harmful investments, our translation) of Intesa Sanpaolo, the museum's owner (LaPresse).

Red flags: 2026 privacy fines against the owner
- In March 2026 Italy's data protection authority, the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, fined Intesa Sanpaolo 17.6 million euros for unlawfully processing the data of about 2.4 million customers moved to its digital bank Isybank (Il Post, 13 March 2026).
- The Garante also fined it 31.8 million euros after an employee made more than 6,600 unauthorised accesses to the data of 3,573 customers between 21 February 2022 and 24 April 2024 (QuiFinanza, 31 March 2026).

WIDER CONTEXT
- ReCommon, an environmental group, said on 29 April 2025 that Intesa's lending to coal, oil and gas rose 18% in 2024 to about $11 billion and its investments rose 16% to about $10 billion; it also pressed the bank over a possible role in ENI's Coral North and Rovuma LNG gas projects in Mozambique, which UniCredit had said it would not finance (L'Indipendente, reporting ReCommon's figures).
- These are the investments the 2025 Extinction Rebellion protest inside the Turin museum objected to.

BOTTOM LINE
A bank's own museums, paid for from its balance sheet. The weak point is the owner itself, whose conduct as a bank has drawn regulators' fines and protest at the museum.
