MANSUDAE ART STUDIO: Pyongyang, North Korea
ID: mansudae-art-studio-pyongyang
Website: https://korean-books.com.kp (checked 2026-10-24; fails the secure connection, certificate could not be verified; the studio's other address, mansudaeartstudio.com, also failed the secure handshake)

OVERVIEW
- State-run art production complex in the Phyongchon District of Pyongyang, over 120,000 square metres, about 80,000 of them indoors (Wikipedia).
- Founded 17 November 1959. About 4,000 staff, roughly 1,000 of them artists, in 13 production groups (woodcuts, charcoal, ceramics, embroidery, jewel painting and others) and more than 50 supply departments (Wikipedia).
- Produces most of North Korea's public art, including monuments, murals and paintings (Koryo Tours).
- Its overseas arm, the Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies, was set up in the 1970s and had made monuments for 18 African and Asian countries by 2014 (Wikipedia).
- Abroad it has run a gallery in Beijing's 798 art district since 2009 and sold through the Italian dealer Pier Luigi Cecioni, whose relationship with the studio began in 2005 (CBS News, 2018).
- Visiting: Koryo Tours (guide of 7 February 2019) says the studio "is on the UN Sanction list and cannot be visited nor art purchased"; before sanctions tourists could tour the site, gallery and studios.

FUNDING
Mansudae Art Studio: state-run, earning foreign currency from art sales and overseas monument contracts
- State institution: Yonhap calls it the state-run North Korean art institution; CBS News calls it a state-run arts enterprise.
- Art sales: Vice (2013) estimated about $160 million earned over the previous decade, largely from production for international clients, and wrote that "all the art sales go straight to the state". Cecioni told Vice the studio has "perhaps unexpected economic autonomy" and that sales money goes to Mansudae itself. He told Reuters that revenues pay for paints and equipment.
- Monuments abroad: CBS News puts Africa monument revenue at an estimated $260 million as of 2016. A UN report said the overseas arm exported workers for construction, generating revenue for the North Korean government or the Workers' Party of Korea (CBS News, OpenSanctions).
- Buyers: Italian dealers, Chinese galleries (Dandong paintings sold for up to $100,000, with dealer mark-ups of four or five times; Reuters) and foreign governments that commission monuments.
- No reporting found of the studio's budget or of a line in the state or party budget.

Red flags: UN, US and other sanctions
- 2016: the UN Security Council banned North Korean statue exports, the studio's monument business (Reuters via Taipei Times; unscr.com).
- 5 August 2017: the UN Security Council blacklisted Mansudae Art Studio with an asset freeze and travel ban (Reuters via Taipei Times).
- 7 December 2016: US Treasury (OFAC) listed "Mansudae Overseas Project Group of Companies (a.k.a. Mansudae Art Studio)" as blocked under Executive Order 13722 (Federal Register).
- November 2018: South Korean business executives on a Pyongyang visit bought paintings at the studio; customs seized about 20 at Incheon airport. The Unification Ministry said it would decide under related laws whether this breached sanctions (UPI, January 2019).
- 2018 Gwangju Biennale: 22 works from a collection held by the studio's Beijing branch and the Yedo Arts Foundation were shown with Ministry of Unification approval (Korea Times, Yonhap). Hyperallergic says the Biennale Foundation did not explain sufficiently how it sourced the works.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Little direct criticism. Art media report the sanctions and the dealers' claims; the one critique found questions how a biennale sourced the works.
1. Hyperallergic: Hayoung Chung, "Curators Strive to Transcend National Borders at the Gwangju Biennale" (2018)
URL: https://hyperallergic.com/imagined-borders-gwangju-biennale-2018
- Finds the North Korean section hard to call a complete success, because labels and catalogue give only titles, names and dates.
- Says the Biennale Foundation has not explained sufficiently how it sourced the works and that Korean media questioned the authenticity and provenance of some pieces.
- Names Mansudae Art Studio; does not mention sanctions.
2. Hyperallergic: Zachary Small, "The Italian Gallery Brokering an International Market for North Korean Art" (2018)
URL: https://hyperallergic.com/442425/pier-luigi-cecioni-mansudae-studio/
- Reports the UN view that Mansudae's profits feed the North Korean government, against Cecioni's claim that his payments go back into running the studio; Cecioni holds over 300 pieces in storage because sanctions limit his imports.
- Not criticism: it records Cecioni's statements without independent checks.
3. VICE: Nadja Sayej, "Behind Mansudae, the Most Prominent Art Institution in North Korea" (2013)
URL: https://vice.com/en/article/behind-mansudae-the-most-prominent-art-institution-in-north-korea
- Says artists get no return on sales and that all sales go to the state; Cecioni says the money stays with the studio. Interview-led profile, not a critique; predates the 2017 listing.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the studio is not clean: it is a state institution on UN and US sanctions lists, and its earnings are tied by the UN to the North Korean government and the Workers' Party. The weak points are the disputed claims about where sales money goes, the continued sales through dealers and the 2018 Gwangju loan. Worth watching: further sales or loans to institutions abroad.
