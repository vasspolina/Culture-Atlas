KOREAN CENTRAL HISTORY MUSEUM: Pyongyang, North Korea
ID: korean-central-history-museum-pyongyang
Website: No site of the museum found (checked 2026-10-24; the Atlas record lists none, and searches returned only Koryo Tours and Wikipedia pages)

OVERVIEW
- National history museum at the north end of Kim Il Sung Square, Pyongyang, in one of the neoclassical buildings round the square (Wikipedia, Koryo Tours).
- Established on 1 December 1945 by Kim Il Sung, originally on Moran Hill; Koryo Tours says it opened in the Moranbong district in 1945 (Wikipedia, Koryo Tours).
- Wikipedia says the first building was destroyed in the Korean War, which it attributes to US troops, and the museum was rebuilt on the square in 1960, with 1977 given as an alternative date; Koryo Tours says it moved there in 1977.
- Wikipedia counts 19 rooms, 10,500 square metres and about 100,000 relics; Koryo Tours says "over 10,000 m2" in 19 halls, as an official figure, and over 4,000 objects. The two sources do not agree on the number of objects.
- Displays run from the Old Stone Age to the March First Uprising of 1919, including a mock-up of the Tomb of Tangun and the "Lolang Basket" (Koryo Tours). Koryo Tours says politicised lessons on the struggle against invaders, oppression and revolution are woven in, and calls the evidence for Tangun "supposed".
- Wikipedia says that in 2014 Kim Jong Un wanted it to serve as a base standard for history museums, and that the museum claimed 10 million visitors since 1945 by 2015; both claims come from state-linked media.
- Visiting: open to foreign tourists; photography inside is prohibited; a small shop sells ceramics and celadon (Koryo Tours, 21 October 2020). No reporting found of opening days or admission.

Red flags: sanctions on the North Korean government
- Executive Order 13722, signed by President Obama on 15 March 2016, blocks property of the Government of North Korea and the Workers' Party of Korea within US jurisdiction. It defines the Government of North Korea as including its "agencies, instrumentalities, and controlled entities" (Executive Order text, American Presidency Project).
- No reporting found that the museum itself is named on a sanctions list.

BOTTOM LINE
No private funder is found behind the museum; the Atlas classes it as a North Korean state museum, which brings the US sanctions order. The weak points are the lack of any published budget or governance and the museum's reliance on state media for its figures. Worth watching: any independent count of its collection and visitors.
