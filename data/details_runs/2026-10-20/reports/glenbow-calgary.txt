GLENBOW: Calgary, Canada
ID: glenbow-calgary
Website: https://www.glenbow.org/ (checked 2026-10-20)

OVERVIEW
- Art museum, archive and gallery at 130 9 Avenue SE in downtown Calgary, in an eight-storey building across from the Calgary Tower (Glenbow, Wikipedia).
- Eric Lafferty Harvie set it up as a private foundation from his own collection in 1955; on 15 April 1966 Alberta's legislature created the Glenbow-Alberta Institute when he gave the collection to the province (Wikipedia).
- Named after Harvie's ranch and a Canadian Pacific Railway station near Cochrane, Alberta (Wikipedia).
- The current building opened on 22 September 1976; renovation began after the museum closed on 29 August 2021, with DIALOG as architects and a 13,000-square-foot rooftop terrace by Maya Lin. The building will be renamed the JR Shaw Centre for Arts & Culture (Glenbow, Wikipedia).
- Holds more than 250,000 artworks, objects and belongings; the reopened museum plans 44 exhibitions and displays on eight floors, visible storage, a conservation lab, a new Blackfoot Gallery curated with Elders from Siksika, Piikani, Kainai and Blackfeet Nation, a theatre, shop and restaurant (stated by the space).
- Closed for renovation, with reopening targeted for 2027; general admission will be free under the $25 million JR Shaw Free Admission Endowment (stated by the space).

FUNDING
Glenbow: mixed: three levels of government for the rebuild, the Shaw family, and Calgary private and energy-sector donors
- Donors of $35 million or more to March 2025: the City of Calgary, the Government of Alberta, the Government of Canada and the Shaw Family Foundation (stated by the space).
- Government capital: $40 million each from Canada and Alberta and $14 million from Calgary, against a $115 million capital cost, with $40 million more sought privately (Wikipedia).
- The Shaw Family Foundation gave $35 million: $25 million for the free admission endowment and $10 million for the JR Shaw Institute for Art in Canada (stated by the space). Board member Heather Shaw, Executive Chair of Corus Entertainment, is a director of that foundation (stated by the space).
- Other named donors to March 2025: Al Osten and Buddy Victor ($5 million+), David Werklund ($3 million+), BMO Financial Group, RBC Foundation, Lori Egger and Steve Laut ($1 million+ each tier), Power Corporation of Canada ($500,000+), Scotiabank and Canada Life ($250,000+), TD Bank Group ($25,000+) and Chevron Canada Resources ($1,000 to $4,999) (stated by the space).
- Imperial Oil gave a $4 million package in February 2006: its corporate archives, a $1.2 million endowment for them and $500,000 for a gallery (CSRwire press release).
- The Devonian Foundation, the Harvie family foundation renamed in 1973, appoints two of the Board of Governors (Glenbow, Legal Archives Society of Alberta).

Red flags: oil money from the founder, the board and donors
- Harvie's fortune came from oil found on land he leased to Imperial Oil at Leduc and Redwater in 1947 and 1948 (Legal Archives Society of Alberta, Wikipedia).
- Board vice chair John R. Whelan, who chairs the Project Oversight Committee for the rebuild, is Chairman, President and CEO of Imperial Oil and a former Exxon Mobil senior vice president (stated by the space). He and Michelle Bailey gave $100,000 to $249,999 (stated by the space).
- Governor Kent Ferguson was Senior Vice President, Strategy, Sustainability and Corporate Development at Suncor (stated by the space).
- Donor Steve Laut became Executive Vice-Chairman of Canadian Natural Resources in March 2018 after serving as president from 2005 (Fort McMurray Chamber).
- Donor David Werklund founded Dave's Oilfield Service, Concord Well Servicing and Canadian Crude Separators, a crude oil processing and waste business (Government of Alberta).

Red flags: 1988 boycott over Shell sponsorship
- The Lubicon Lake Cree boycotted Glenbow's exhibition for the 1988 Calgary Winter Olympics, first titled "Forget Not My Land" and sponsored by Shell, one of the oil companies Alberta let drill on Lubicon land (Cultural Survival Quarterly, James G.E. Smith).
- Museums backing the boycott included the Museum of the American Indian, Harvard's Peabody Museum, the National Museum of Denmark and the Horniman Museum (Cultural Survival Quarterly).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: No criticism found in art media.

BOTTOM LINE
Most of the rebuild money is public, and the largest private gift is Shaw media money. The weak point is oil: the founder's fortune, Imperial Oil's current CEO as board vice chair overseeing the rebuild, and oil executives among the larger donors. The 1988 Shell boycott shows the issue is not new.
