ART GALLERY OF SOUTH AUSTRALIA: Adelaide, Australia
ID: art-gallery-of-south-australia-adelaide
Website: https://www.agsa.sa.gov.au/ (checked 2026-10-20)

OVERVIEW
- State art museum at 490 North Terrace, Adelaide, in the cultural precinct between the South Australian Museum and Adelaide University.
- Founded in 1881 as the National Gallery of South Australia; renamed the Art Gallery of South Australia in 1967 (Wikipedia).
- The Classical Revival Elder Wing by C. E. Owen Smyth opened in 1900, with a Doric portico added in 1936 and extensions in 1962, 1979 and 1996; the building is on the South Australian Heritage Register (Wikipedia).
- The collection holds almost 45,000 works, the second-largest state collection in Australia; the gallery runs the Adelaide Biennial of Australian Art (since 1990) and Tarnanthi, a festival of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander art (since 2015).
- Jason Smith has been director since February 2025, after Rhana Devenport (2018 to 2024) (Wikipedia).
- Open 10:00 to 17:00 daily except Christmas Day; general entry is free, some exhibitions charge; free wheelchair loan, accessible parking nearby, assistance dogs welcome.

FUNDING
Art Gallery of South Australia: mainly public: South Australian government, with BHP as Tarnanthi's corporate partner
- The Government of South Australia funds the gallery, which reports to the Department of the Premier and Cabinet since 2018, and is principal partner of Tarnanthi (Wikipedia, AGSA).
- BHP is Tarnanthi's presenting partner (AGSA) and has backed it since 2015; the gallery called BHP a "resources industry rather than a fossil fuel industry" and says it tells exhibiting artists about the funding under a "continuous consent" model (InDaily).
- Bequests: Thomas Elder left £25,000 in 1897; the estate of James and Diana Ramsay gave A$38 million, announced in 2019 (Wikipedia).
- No reporting found that could be opened giving the amount of the BHP deal or its 2026 terms; the annual report and parliamentary records returned 403 Forbidden.

Red flags: BHP and the 2015 Samarco dam collapse
- The Fundão dam in south-eastern Brazil collapsed in 2015 and killed 19 people (AAP).
- On 14 November 2025 London's High Court, Judge Finola O'Farrell, found BHP liable though it did not own the dam: raising the dam when it was unsafe was the "direct and immediate cause" of the collapse (AAP).
- The claimants' lawyers value the claim at up to US$48.32 billion; BHP said it would appeal and that 240,000 claimants had been paid in Brazil (AAP).
- BHP is current: the gallery's Tarnanthi page names it presenting partner (checked October 2026); it also keeps some coal interests (InDaily).
- No reporting found of an artist protest, boycott or open letter against BHP at the gallery.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Little criticism. One arts-section feature puts Tarnanthi's BHP deal inside an Adelaide debate on "artwashing"; no piece found makes a direct case against the gallery.
1. InDaily (InReview): Farrin Foster, "The 'artwashing' debate arrives in Adelaide" (2023)
URL: https://www.indailysa.com.au/inreview/archive/2023/04/21/the-artwashing-debate-arrives-in-adelaide
- Calls Tarnanthi one of the most visible partnerships between an extractive company and a cultural body in South Australia.
- Mirning artist Ali Gumilya Baker objects to companies profiting from Aboriginal land while returning little, and to opaque deals; documentarian Alex Kelly warns that dependence on funders breeds self-censorship.
- Gives the gallery's answer: Nici Cumpston credits BHP with 1.6 million attendees, over 6,000 artists shown and over $6.6 million in art fair sales.
- The open letter it reports, signed by more than 60 arts figures in January 2023, targets Santos at the Adelaide Botanic Garden, not this gallery.

BOTTOM LINE
The gallery's core money is state government funding and bequests. The weak point is BHP: the corporate partner behind Tarnanthi, a festival of First Nations art, was found liable in 2025 for a dam collapse that killed 19 people. Worth watching: the value and terms of BHP's next deal, and whether artists act on the "artwashing" debate.
