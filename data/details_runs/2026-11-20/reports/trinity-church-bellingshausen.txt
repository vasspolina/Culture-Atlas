HOLY TRINITY CHURCH: King George Island, Antarctica
ID: trinity-church-bellingshausen
Website: none (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Small Russian Orthodox church on King George Island, beside Russia's Bellingshausen research station; a wooden building 15 m high that holds up to 30 worshippers (Wikipedia).
- By his own account, the idea came from Valery Lukin, head of the Russian Antarctic Expedition at the Arctic and Antarctic Research Institute in St Petersburg. He said he first planned a chapel at the Mirny station cemetery and that Patriarch Alexius II proposed a full church instead, at Bellingshausen, which tourist ships often visit (Lukin, quoted in Parlamentskaya Gazeta, 19 February 2016; translated from Russian).
- Architects P.I. Anisifirov, S.G. Rybak and A.B. Schmidt of Barnaul won the design competition; Altai carpenters led by K.V. Khromov built it, and station staff reassembled it under Father Kallistrat (Romanenko), its first priest (Wikipedia). It was first raised in Siberian cedar and larch in the Altai mountains, shipped in pieces and assembled on site between December 2003 and January 2004 (Lukin in Russian Polar Research, 2014).
- Bishop Theognost, abbot of the Trinity Lavra of St Sergius, performed a small consecration on 14 February 2004 and Bishop Iakov of Naryan-Mar, sent by Patriarch Kirill, the great consecration on 14 February 2014 (Russian Polar Research, 2014; Wikipedia dates the 2004 consecration to 15 February). Palekh painters made the iconostasis, and descendants of Sergey Muravyov-Apostol commissioned the bells (Wikipedia).
- One or two monks of the Trinity Lavra serve it year round, rotated yearly; they pray for the Russians who died in Antarctic expeditions and help maintain the station. Staff of the Chilean, Polish and Korean stations and tourists attend, and some services are held in Spanish; the first wedding took place on 29 January 2007 (Wikipedia).

FUNDING
Holy Trinity Church: private donations raised under the Russian Orthodox Church's patronage, staffed by the Trinity Lavra
- Built with donations collected across Russia by the charity "Temple for Antarctica", which Patriarch Alexius II approved (Wikipedia).
- In the same article Lukin said he told his friend Pyotr Zadirov of the plan and Zadirov offered to finance the project. This is Lukin's account alone, in a paper that is the official weekly of Russia's Federal Assembly (Parlamentskaya Gazeta; translated from Russian). In his 2014 note for his own institute's journal Lukin calls Zadirov a Moscow businessman whose plan with Lukin Alexius II backed in 2000, and names him as the financier of the church's tenth-anniversary album (Russian Polar Research; translated from Russian).
- The Russian Geographical Society and RusHydro gave organisational support to the 2014 consecration mission (Russian Polar Research).
- That article answered claims made online during Patriarch Kirill's 2016 visit that the church had been built and shipped with state budget money; its headline says the church was built with private money (Parlamentskaya Gazeta).

Red flags: UK sanctions on Patriarch Kirill since 2022
- On 16 June 2022 the UK sanctioned Patriarch Kirill, head of the Russian Orthodox Church, "for his support and endorsement of Putin's war" (UK government).
- Kirill flew to Bellingshausen from Punta Arenas on 17 February 2016 to hold a service in the church; his spokesman Aleksandr Volkov called the trip "a proof that the Russian Orthodox Church exercises its office on all continents" (RFE/RL; The Moscow Times).

BOTTOM LINE
The building money is private but thinly documented. The red flag is leadership, not money: the church answers to a sanctioned head. Worth watching: further visits by the church's leadership.
