TEYLERS MUSEUM: Haarlem, Netherlands
ID: teylers-museum
Website: https://teylersmuseum.nl/en (checked 2026-11-20)

OVERVIEW
- Museum of art and science at Spaarne 16 in Haarlem, with fossils, scientific instruments, books, coins, prints and drawings.
- Named for Pieter Teyler (1702 to 1778), a Haarlem silk merchant and banker, out of whose will it grew and whose foundation was run by five of his friends. The Oval Room opened in 1784, which makes it the oldest museum in the Netherlands (stated by the space).
- Teylers Stichting, the foundation set up by his will, owns the buildings and the collections.
- The restored Lorentz Lab, where the Nobel laureate Lorentz worked for almost twenty years, opened in May 2017.
- The museum had 171,630 visitors in 2024, the second-highest figure in its history (annual report 2024).
- Open Tuesday to Sunday 10:00 to 17:00, closed Monday. Adults pay EUR 18.50 and ages 18 to 24 EUR 9.25.

FUNDING
Teylers Museum: mixed: a state subsidy for about a third, the rest from tickets, friends, funds and a lottery
- Income in 2024 was EUR 11,242,646: EUR 3,860,901 in subsidy from the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science, EUR 110,200 in other public subsidies and EUR 7,064,045 in own income (annual report 2024).
- Own income is admissions, room hire and contributions from friends' funds and private funds for projects. The year included a bequest of almost EUR 4 million that the donor required to be kept as capital.
- The other public money is from the Restauratiefonds, the Mondriaan Fonds and a yearly contribution from the municipality of Haarlem.
- The report lists the VriendenLoterij as main benefactor and Teylers Stichting, the Teylers Museum Vrienden Fonds and the Netherlands America Foundation as structural benefactors.
- Exhibitions in 2024 had support from Het Cultuurfonds, Fonds 21 and Zabawas, and the Province of Noord-Holland supported the collections.
- Expenses in 2024 were EUR 7,852,050, of which EUR 4,237,427 was staff.

WIDER CONTEXT
- The museum's own annual report says Pieter Teyler's surviving accounts show that part of his investments was related to slavery.
- His fortune founded Teylers Stichting, which still owns the museum's buildings and collections.

BOTTOM LINE
The money is a ministry subsidy, a public lottery, friends and Dutch cultural funds. The point to know is historical: the museum itself states that part of its founder's investments was tied to slavery.
