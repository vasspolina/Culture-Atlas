WELLCOME COLLECTION: London, UK
ID: wellcome-collection
Website: https://wellcome.org (checked 2026-10-24; the museum's own site is https://wellcomecollection.org)

OVERVIEW
- Free museum and library at 183 Euston Road, London, near Euston station.
- Opened to the public in June 2007, as part of the Wellcome Trust (Wikipedia). It follows an earlier Wellcome Historical Medical Museum at 54a Wigmore Street (Wikipedia).
- Named for Sir Henry Solomon Wellcome (1853 to 1936), the pharmaceutical entrepreneur and collector whose will created the Wellcome Trust and left it his collection of historical medical items (Wellcome Trust).
- Building: the Wellcome Building on Euston Road was opened to the public after the Trust moved its offices into the Gibbs Building, designed by Michael Hopkins and Partners and completed in 2004. A £17.5 million transformation by Wilkinson Eyre reopened it in October 2015 (Wikipedia).
- On site: galleries, a library, a café and a shop. The collection holds rare books, artworks, films, personal archives and objects (stated by the museum).
- Visiting: exhibitions, events and library access are free. Galleries are open 10:00 to 18:00 and the library 10:00 to 16:00 on the day checked. Step-free access and hearing loops are provided, and BSL-interpreted and Deaf-led tours are offered (stated by the museum).

FUNDING
Wellcome Collection: endowed: the Wellcome Trust's investment portfolio, about £38 billion
- Part of Wellcome, a charitable foundation. The Trust says it is not funded by GSK or any other pharmaceutical company and pays for its work from its investment portfolio, worth around £38 billion (Wellcome Trust).
- The endowment began with Henry Wellcome's will in 1936, which left the Trust his pharmaceutical company. Wellcome plc was sold to Glaxo from 1995 (Wellcome Trust).
- No reporting found of any donor, sponsor or public grant paying for the Collection itself.

Red flags: Wellcome Trust fossil-fuel investments
- Campaign: in March 2015 The Guardian launched a petition, signed by more than 93,000 people, asking the Wellcome Trust and the Gates Foundation to divest from the top 200 fossil fuel companies within five years (Civil Society).
- Response: the Trust said it took climate change seriously in its investment decisions and engaged with the businesses it invests in (Civil Society, 2015).
- Sale: in 2022 Wellcome sold its BP and Shell shares, saying the sales moved holdings to non-cyclical industries and were "not a reflection of the companies' approach to the energy transition" (Civil Society, 21 July 2022). The stake's size and any remaining fossil fuel holdings are not stated there.
- The campaign targeted the Trust, not the museum.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Criticism of the Collection exists but sits mostly in general-press and academic pieces, not specialist art outlets. It comes from two opposite sides: that the Collection did too little to confront Wellcome's colonial collecting, and that it now distances itself too far from its founder.
1. The Conversation: Anaïs Walsdorf, "Why Wellcome closed its Medicine Man exhibition, and others should follow suit" (2022)
URL: https://theconversation.com/why-wellcome-closed-its-medicine-man-exhibition-and-others-should-follow-suit-196171
- Argues the gallery told history from a narrow Eurocentric view and displayed objects without the violent circumstances of their acquisition, and that the closure came late.
- Cites Wellcome's own anti-racism evaluation finding "insufficient progress" and discrimination experienced by staff.
- The author is a former Wellcome Collection employee.
2. The Spectator: Ross Clark, "What has become of the Wellcome Collection?" (2024)
URL: https://spectator.com/?p=516506
- Argues the Hard Graft exhibition is multimedia art, not the scholarly show the Collection once staged.
- Argues the Collection pushes fashionable ideology and distances itself from its founder after the Medicine Man closure, and defends Henry Wellcome against being called an "evil colonialist".
- An opinion column.

WIDER CONTEXT
- The Guardian's 2015 petition and the 2022 sale of BP and Shell shares concern the Trust's investments, not the museum.
- The Collection is part of the Trust and is paid for from the same endowment.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the Collection is the Wellcome Trust's single endowment, built on a pharmaceutical firm and no named outside donor. The weak points are the Trust's fossil-fuel holdings, which drew a documented 2015 campaign and were partly sold in 2022, and the Collection's colonial-era acquisitions. Worth watching: the Trust's remaining fossil fuel investments, which no source opened here states.
