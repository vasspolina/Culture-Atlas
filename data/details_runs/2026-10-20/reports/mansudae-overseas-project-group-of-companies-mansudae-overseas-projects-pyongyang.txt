MANSUDAE OVERSEAS PROJECT GROUP OF COMPANIES (MANSUDAE OVERSEAS PROJECTS): Pyongyang, North Korea
ID: mansudae-overseas-project-group-of-companies-mansudae-overseas-projects-pyongyang
Website: none found (checked 2026-10-24)

OVERVIEW
- Pyongyang construction company that is the overseas commercial arm of the Mansudae Art Studio. It builds monuments, memorials and public buildings, mainly in Africa and Southeast Asia, using North Korean artists, engineers and construction workers (Wikipedia).
- Set up in the 1970s; projects are listed in 17 countries as of 2015, including Angola, Egypt, Ethiopia, Germany, Malaysia, Namibia, Senegal and Zimbabwe (Wikipedia).
- Main works: the African Renaissance Monument in Dakar, unveiled in 2010; Namibia's Heroes' Acre (inaugurated August 2002), State House (2008) and Independence Memorial Museum (2014); the Angkor Panorama Museum in Cambodia, closed indefinitely in 2020 (Wikipedia).
- Visiting: a contractor, not a venue. Its monuments are public sites in the countries where they stand.

FUNDING
Mansudae Overseas Projects: state-owned North Korean company paid by foreign governments and clients under contract
- The UN listing says the group exported workers from North Korea for construction and that this generated revenue for the North Korean government or the Workers' Party of Korea (OpenSanctions, citing the 2017 UN Panel of Experts; CBS News).
- Wikipedia gives an estimated $160 million earned from overseas monument and memorial projects as of August 2011; CBS News estimates Africa monument revenue at $260 million as of 2016.
- Senegal: a US embassy cable of June 2008 (Dakar) put the monument's value at about $30 million and said Senegal would give a North Korean firm land in Almadies worth about $30 million. A March 2009 cable cited a $16 million price tag and called the financing murky. Neither cable names Mansudae; Wikipedia and Daily NK credit the monument to the group. CBS News says the monument cost Senegal $27 million.
- Cambodia: Mansudae designed, built and financed the $24 million Angkor Panorama Museum; profits go to the North Koreans for 10 years, then are split 50-50 with the state body Apsara, then the museum reverts to the Cambodian government (Al Jazeera, 2016).
- Namibia: contracts included Heroes' Acre (inaugurated 2002), State House (built 2002 to 2008) and the Independence Memorial Museum (opened 2014) (Daily NK). No reporting found of the contract totals.
- No reporting found of accounts, owners or a board.

Red flags: UN, US, EU, UK and other sanctions
- 5 August 2017: the group was listed by the UN Security Council; OpenSanctions also records US (OFAC), EU and UK listings with the same start date and an Australian listing dated 20 February 2026.
- The UN Panel of Experts (2017) recommended designation covering "all Mansudae operations and subsidiary branches world-wide" (OpenSanctions).
- 7 December 2016: US Treasury listed it among entities blocked under Executive Order 13722 (Federal Register).
- Namibia: in February 2016 the UN Panel of Experts concluded that Namibia's dealings with Mansudae violated sanctions, citing a munitions factory at Leopard Valley, a military academy and the Ministry of Defence headquarters. Namibia said the factory was finished in 2005; the Panel found a 2010 Mansudae document advertising a research centre there and satellite images showing building until 2014 (African Arguments, 2016).
- Cambodia: human rights groups worry the Angkor museum's income could help circumvent sanctions (Al Jazeera).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Art press coverage is mostly descriptive or tonal, not a full case against the company; the sharpest political framing comes from an artist-author at MoMA's online platform.
1. The Art Newspaper: unsigned, "North Korea's dubious gifts to African public art" (2015)
URL: https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2015/05/11/north-koreas-dubious-gifts-to-african-public-art
- Calls the monuments "overbearing, blingtastic" and says North Korea has a near-monopoly on large public monuments in sub-Saharan Africa.
- Names the group as builder of Heroes' Acre in Windhoek and the Dakar monument; the criticism is implied through tone and framing, not argued outright.
2. MoMA post: Onejoon Che, "Mansudae Master Class: The Monumental Gifts from North Korea" (2016)
URL: https://post.moma.org/?p=3483
- Says the group began sending artists and labourers abroad for foreign currency after the mid-1990s, and that the projects often honour African dictatorships, which raises suspicion of ties to North Korea's nuclear programme.
- Argues the structures could be "a portal to greater understanding" of Korean peninsula history, so it is a mixed assessment rather than an attack.

WIDER CONTEXT
- US embassy cables from Dakar (2008, 2009) called President Wade's monument a "white elephant" with a non-transparent land arrangement and ties to a "pariah state" (WikiLeaks-published cables). The cables do not name Mansudae.
- The group is the North Korean builder of that monument (Wikipedia, Daily NK).

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the company is not clean: it is a state revenue-raiser listed by the UN, US, EU and UK, and a UN panel found its Namibian work breached arms-related sanctions. The weak points are opaque payments such as the Senegal land arrangement, and contracts with governments that continue to commission monuments. Worth watching: Cambodia, where income is shared with a state body, and any state that still awards contracts.
