WELLCOME COLLECTION: London, UK
ID: wellcome-collection
Website: https://wellcome.org (checked 2026-10-24; the museum's own site is https://wellcomecollection.org)

OVERVIEW
- Free museum and library at 183 Euston Road, London.
- Opened to the public in June 2007 and is part of the Wellcome Trust (Wikipedia). It follows an earlier Wellcome Historical Medical Museum at 54a Wigmore Street (Wikipedia).
- Named for Sir Henry Wellcome (1853 to 1936, Wikipedia), whose will created the Wellcome Trust and left it his pharmaceutical company and his collection of historical medical items (Wellcome Trust).
- Building: the Trust moved its administrative offices into the new Gibbs Building, designed by Michael Hopkins and Partners and completed in 2004. The Collection reopened with additional public spaces in October 2015 after a £17.5 million refurbishment, a transformation by Wilkinson Eyre (Wikipedia).
- On site: galleries, a library, a café and a shop. The collection holds rare books, artworks, films and videos, personal archives and objects on health and medicine (stated by the museum).
- Visiting: exhibitions, events and library access are free. Galleries are open 10:00 to 18:00 and the library 10:00 to 16:00 on the day checked. Step-free access and hearing loops are provided, and BSL-interpreted and Deaf-led BSL tours run for major exhibitions (stated by the museum).

FUNDING
Wellcome Collection: endowed: the Wellcome Trust's investment portfolio, about £38 billion
- Part of Wellcome, a charitable foundation. The Trust says it is not funded by GSK or any other pharmaceutical company and pays for its work from its investment portfolio, worth around £38 billion (Wellcome Trust).
- The endowment began with Henry Wellcome's will in 1936, which left the Trust his pharmaceutical company. Wellcome plc was sold to Glaxo from 1995 (Wellcome Trust).
- No reporting found of any donor, sponsor or public grant paying for the Collection itself.

Red flags: Wellcome Trust fossil-fuel investments
- Campaign: on 19 March 2015 The Guardian launched a petition, signed by more than 93,000 people, asking the Wellcome Trust and the Gates Foundation to divest from the top 200 fossil fuel companies within five years and to freeze new investments in them (Civil Society).
- Holdings in 2015: Civil Society reported that the Trust's direct equity holdings included Shell and BP, together worth £260 million. It gave no share of the endowment.
- Response: the Trust called climate change "one of the greatest contemporary challenges facing our society" and "highly complex", and said it engaged with policymakers, researchers and the businesses it invests in (Civil Society, 2015).
- Sale: in 2022 Wellcome sold its BP and Shell shares, saying it was concentrating its holdings in "non-cyclical industries" and that the sales were "not a reflection of the companies' approach to the energy transition" (Civil Society, 21 July 2022). That article gives no stake size, share of the portfolio or remaining fossil fuel holdings.
- The campaign targeted the Trust, not the museum.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: Criticism of the Collection exists but sits mostly in general-press and academic pieces, not specialist art outlets. It comes from two opposite sides: that the Collection did too little to confront Wellcome's colonial collecting, and that it distances itself too far from its founder.
1. The Conversation: Anaïs Walsdorf, "Why Wellcome closed its Medicine Man exhibition, and others should follow suit" (2022)
URL: https://theconversation.com/why-wellcome-closed-its-medicine-man-exhibition-and-others-should-follow-suit-196171
- Argues the gallery told history from a narrow Eurocentric view centred on Henry Wellcome, with little context on how objects were acquired, and that museums should go beyond adding perspectives to displays.
- Says a summer 2022 report found that Wellcome perpetuated "systematic racism" and described discrimination faced by staff.
- The author is a former Wellcome Collection employee.
2. The Spectator: Ross Clark, "What has become of the Wellcome Collection?" (2024)
URL: https://spectator.com/?p=516506
- Argues the Hard Graft exhibition is multimedia art, not the scholarly show the Collection once staged.
- Criticises the Collection for distancing itself from its founder, whom he says it dismisses as an "evil colonialist".
- An opinion-driven arts review in the 5 October 2024 magazine issue.

WIDER CONTEXT
- The Guardian's 2015 petition and the 2022 sale of BP and Shell shares concern the Trust's investments, not the museum.
- The Collection is part of the Trust and is paid for from the same endowment.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the Collection is the Wellcome Trust's single endowment, built on a pharmaceutical firm and no named outside donor. The weak points are the Trust's fossil-fuel holdings, which drew a documented 2015 campaign and included BP and Shell shares sold in 2022, and the Collection's colonial-era acquisitions. Worth watching: the Trust's remaining fossil fuel investments, which no page opened states.
