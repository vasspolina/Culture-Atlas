OVERVIEW
- National collection of portraits at St Martin's Place, London.
- Proposed by Philip Stanhope, 5th Earl Stanhope, in 1846 and opened in 1856; it moved to St Martin's Place in 1896 (Wikipedia).
- The St Martin's Place building was designed by Ewan Christian, with a 1933 wing funded by Lord Duveen and the 2000 Ondaatje Wing by Edward Jones and Jeremy Dixon (Wikipedia).
- The Inspiring People refurbishment closed the building from 2020 to June 2023; the project target was 35.5 million pounds and Wikipedia gives its cost as 41 million.
- No reporting found of current hours, admission or visitor numbers on the pages read; the Gallery's site refused automated requests.

FUNDING
National Portrait Gallery: mixed: government grant-in-aid, private gifts and corporate partners
- In 2025-26 grant-in-aid from the Department for Culture, Media and Sport was 11,009,000 pounds (10,986,000 the year before) and other trading activities brought in 5,012,000 pounds (annual report 2025-26).
- Corporate partner: Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer, a supporter for about 20 years, now title sponsor of the Portrait Award in place of BP; Extinction Rebellion objected because its oil and gas team acts for BP and Chevron (The Art Newspaper, 15 November 2023).
- Major gifts: the Blavatnik Family Foundation gave 10 million pounds in 2022, Randy Lerner 5 million in 2008, and the Heritage Lottery Fund 9.4 million in 2017 toward the refurbishment (Wikipedia).
- No reporting found of other current corporate supporters on the pages read.

Red flags: BP sponsorship of the Portrait Award (ended 2022)
- BP sponsored the Portrait Award from 1990, sat on its judging panel for 23 years and withdrew from the panel in 2020. In February 2022 BP said the deal would not be renewed when it ended at the close of 2022 (The Art Newspaper, 22 February 2022).
- In 2019 members of BP or not BP? attached themselves to the main gates, and the artist Gary Hume wrote an open letter, co-signed by 80 artists, urging the Gallery to end the deal (The Art Newspaper for the gates, Hyperallergic for the letter).
- BP's UK head, Louise Kingham, spoke of becoming net zero by 2050. Jess Worth of Culture Unstained, a campaign group, called the end "clearly a vote of no confidence in BP's business" and said BP still invested in oil and gas (The Art Newspaper).

Red flags: Sackler gift refused (2019)
- The Sackler Trust withdrew a 1 million pound gift to the 35.5 million pound refurbishment; the Gallery and the Trust said they "jointly agreed not to proceed" (Museums Journal, 21 March 2019). Photographer Nan Goldin had said weeks earlier she would pull her retrospective if the gift went ahead.
- The Trust said the allegations against family members were "vigorously denied".

Red flags: Blavatnik Family Foundation gift (oil money)
- The Blavatnik Family Foundation gave 10 million pounds in 2022 toward Inspiring People, and the Gallery named its first floor the Blavatnik Wing (Wikipedia, The Art Newspaper, 15 November 2023).
- Len Blavatnik's Access Industries was a partner in AAR, which bought 40 percent of Tyumen Oil, and Rosneft paid AAR 28 billion dollars in 2013 for its half of TNK-BP (Wikipedia). Blavatnik denies any connection to Vladimir Putin.
- Ukraine's president imposed personal sanctions on Blavatnik in December 2023 (Wikipedia only). The page names no US, EU or UK sanctions.

WIDER CONTEXT
- The NPG exit followed those of Tate (2017), the Royal Shakespeare Company (2019) and the National Galleries of Scotland (2019) from BP (Hyperallergic).
- The Gallery is one of those institutions; BP still sponsors the British Museum.

BOTTOM LINE
Public grant-in-aid was 11 million pounds in 2025-26. BP ended after protest and the Sackler gift was withdrawn. The remaining weak point is the 10 million pound Blavatnik gift, from a donor whose AAR group held Russian oil assets and who Ukraine sanctioned in 2023. Watch the corporate list.
