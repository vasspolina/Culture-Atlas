PROJECT ARTS CENTRE: Dublin, Ireland
ID: project-arts-centre-dublin
Website: https://projectartscentre.ie (checked 2026-10-25; loaded)

OVERVIEW
- Multidisciplinary contemporary arts centre at 39 East Essex Street, Temple Bar, Dublin 2 (projectartscentre.ie).
- Founded in 1967 by Jim FitzGerald and Colm O'Briain after a three-week festival at the Gate Theatre in 1966; Wikipedia calls it the first such arts centre in Ireland. It had several homes before opening in a converted factory on East Essex Street in 1975 (Wikipedia).
- Building: the 1975 building was demolished in 1998 and a purpose-built replacement on the same site, designed by Shay Cleary Architects, opened in 2000 with two auditoriums (200 and 80 seats), a gallery and a bar, as part of the second phase of Temple Bar's regeneration (Wikipedia).
- What is there: theatre, music, dance, performance and visual art. Wikipedia notes it has hosted the Dublin Fringe and Dublin Theatre Festival, and that Eimear Walshe's exhibition "Romantic Ireland," curated by Sara Greavu and the centre, was selected for the Irish Pavilion at the 2024 Venice Biennale.
- Visiting: the box office is open Monday to Saturday 11am to 6pm; the home page links to accessibility pages but gives no details or ticket prices (projectartscentre.ie).

FUNDING
Project Arts Centre: mainly public: the Arts Council, with Dublin City Council, individual donors and ticket sales
- The Arts Council awarded Project €979,600 in Arts Centre Funding in its first round of 2025 decisions, announced 9 January 2025 (Dublin People).
- The centre says it relies on contributions from individuals, corporations and foundations and publishes named supporters in four giving tiers (Director's Circle, Visionaries, Innovators, Pioneers); the supporter list names individuals only, and no company or foundation is named in text (projectartscentre.ie/support-us). It is registered charity CHY6465.
- Project's 2022 annual report says it is "largely funded through a grant from The Arts Council," supplemented by grants from Dublin City Council, fundraising and ticket income. No reporting found of a Dublin City Council grant figure.
- No reporting found of any corporate sponsor or board list.

WIDER CONTEXT
- The Charities Regulator, a state body, is not named as a funder of Project in any source opened; the 2022 annual report names the Arts Council and Dublin City Council. The mural order is therefore not counted as state censorship by a funder.
- In April 2018 the Project said the Charities Regulator considered Maser's "Repeal the 8th" mural "political activity" outside its charitable purpose under the Charities Act 2009, and that the centre risked losing charitable status if the work stayed up (The Irish Times, Kitty Holland, 20 April 2018). The regulator had written to the board on 12 April; a spokesman said it was "not taking sides in any debate" and pointed to guidance published in February (The Irish Times).
- Project painted over the mural on 23 April 2018 before about 200 protesters; artistic director Cian O'Brien said: "You can paint over a mural but you can't paint over an issue" (GCN, Katie Donohoe, 2018). Chair Fiona Slevin said the work fitted the centre's remit (The Irish Times). Andrea Horan of The HunReal Issues, which commissioned the mural, said that if you look hard enough for a rule you will find one (GCN).
- A near-identical Maser mural had been removed in July 2016 after complaints and a Dublin City Council direction that it lacked planning permission (The Irish Times).

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind Project is the Arts Council plus Dublin City Council and individual donors, with no flagged-industry funder found. The weak points are that Project painted over a political mural in 2018 after the Charities Regulator, a state body not named as a funder in any source opened, judged it political activity under charity law (as the Project relayed), and that no corporate supporters or board list are published on the pages read. Worth watching how charity rules on political art are applied.
