COLLECTIVE: Edinburgh, UK
ID: collective-edinburgh
Website: https://www.collective-edinburgh.art/ (checked 2026-10-20)

OVERVIEW
- Free contemporary art centre in the former City Observatory at 38 Calton Hill, Edinburgh (stated by the space).
- Founded in 1984 as the Artist's Collective Gallery by dissenting members of the artist-run 57 Gallery, which was being absorbed into the Fruitmarket Gallery (Wikipedia).
- It was on Cockburn Street until 2013, then in a temporary Calton Hill gallery, and opened the restored observatory site in November 2018 (Wikipedia, The Art Newspaper).
- The A-listed City Observatory of 1818 was designed by William Playfair; its transit telescope gave Leith sailors accurate time (The Art Newspaper).
- The £4 million redevelopment, begun by Malcolm Fraser Architects and finished by Collective Architecture (no relation to the gallery), added two new buildings, the Hillside gallery and the Lookout restaurant (Wikipedia, The Art Newspaper).
- Kate Gray directed it from 2009 to 2021; Sorcha Carey is director (Wikipedia). It also lets Observatory House and an apartment as holiday stays and hires the site for weddings (stated by the space).
- Visiting: open Wednesday to Sunday 10am to 4pm, closed Monday and Tuesday; admission is free (stated by the space).

FUNDING
Collective: mainly public with trusts: City of Edinburgh Council, Creative Scotland and the National Lottery Heritage Fund, plus trusts and site income
- Income was £801,771 and spending £923,678 in the year to 31 March 2025; income was £713,491 the year before (OSCR, charity SC009405).
- The current funder logos are the City of Edinburgh Council, Creative Scotland and the Heritage Fund (stated by the space).
- Programme supporters named for 2026: the National Lottery Heritage Fund, Henry Moore Foundation, Hinrichsen Foundation, Goethe-Institut Glasgow, John Preston Trust, Idlewild Trust and Fenton Arts Trust; some donors are anonymous (stated by the space).
- The redevelopment was paid for with the City of Edinburgh Council, Creative Scotland and the Heritage Lottery Fund, with the Wolfson, Garfield Weston, William Grant and Sylvia Waddilove foundations, the Pilgrim Trust, WREN and others (The Art Newspaper, e-flux).
- No reporting found of the size of Creative Scotland's current grant to Collective.

Red flags: Baillie Gifford sponsorship dropped under pressure
- Collective's 2018 opening exhibition, "Affinity and Allusion", thanked Baillie Gifford's investment managers, the Elephant Trust and the Royal Over-Seas League for supporting it (e-flux).
- Art Workers for Palestine Scotland named Collective among cultural bodies Baillie Gifford funds and asked the firm to divest from Cemex, Cisco Systems and Booking Holdings over business in Israeli settlements (Lighthouse Bookshop guest post by Art Workers for Palestine Scotland, an advocacy group).
- By summer 2024 Collective had announced it would drop Baillie Gifford, which the campaign credits to its organising (Snack, interview with the campaign).
- In August 2025 the campaign listed Collective, with the Book Festival, Fruitmarket and Stills, as Edinburgh bodies that had cut ties with the firm (Morning Star).
- No reporting opened here gives the value of Collective's Baillie Gifford money; the record's Scotsman source could not be read (it refused access).

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: No criticism found in art media. Coverage of the 2018 reopening is admiring, and the Baillie Gifford pressure came from a campaign group, not critics.

WIDER CONTEXT
- Art Workers for Palestine Scotland says Baillie Gifford's holdings fall short of "the commitment to social justice that is expected from Scotland's cultural organisations" (Lighthouse Bookshop guest post); in 2025 it called the Edinburgh International Festival's ties to the firm "indefensible" (Morning Star). Both sources are advocacy.
- Collective took Baillie Gifford money for its opening show and dropped the firm under this campaign.

BOTTOM LINE
Collective's money now comes from the city council, Creative Scotland, lottery heritage money, arts trusts and income from the site itself. Its one funder controversy, Baillie Gifford, ended when it dropped the firm by 2024 under campaign pressure. Worth watching: deficits (spending exceeded income in both 2024 and 2025) and any return of corporate sponsorship.
