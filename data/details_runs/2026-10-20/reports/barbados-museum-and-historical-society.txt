BARBADOS MUSEUM AND HISTORICAL SOCIETY: Bridgetown, Barbados
ID: barbados-museum-and-historical-society
Website: https://barbmuse.org.bb (checked 2026-10-24)

OVERVIEW
- History museum and learned society in the Garrison area of Bridgetown, part of the Historic Bridgetown and its Garrison UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- Formed in 1933 by E.M. Shilstone, L.T. Yearwood and others; the Act of Incorporation passed on 31 May 1933, Sir Frederick Clarke was elected first President on 21 June 1933, and the museum opened to the public on 8 May 1934.
- The lease on the site was signed on 29 November 1933.
- Housed in a former British military prison, which the museum calls "a former British Military Prison that is now the island's leading museum".
- On site: galleries (Jubilee, Harewood, Picture, Coronation, Aall, Children's), the Eustace Maxwell Shilstone library and a shop. The society also owns the Newton Enslaved Burial Ground in Christ Church, bequeathed to it in 1993.
- Visiting: open Monday to Saturday, 9:00 am to 5:00 pm, closed Sundays and bank holidays. Admission prices and accessibility details are not listed on the museum's Visit page.

FUNDING
Barbados Museum and Historical Society: mainly public and self-earned: a Government of Barbados grant plus memberships, gate takings, shop sales and ground rentals
- The Government of Barbados gives the museum an annual grant; main income comes from membership fees, gate receipts, gift shop sales and rental of the grounds, with some consulting and advisory work and training (Visit Barbados).
- The society describes itself as a non-profit, non-governmental organisation with more than 1,000 individual and corporate members (Visit Barbados).
- Government has a seat at the table: the 14-member Council has nine members elected from the membership and five appointed by the government (Visit Barbados). The museum's Council page lists Nicole Alleyne of the Ministry of Tourism and International Transport and Andrea Wells of the National Cultural Foundation among its members.
- Historical grants: the government approved a £250 grant on 16 January 1934; the Carnegie Corporation of New York gave £1,000 in February 1935 and £250 each in November 1935 and July 1936; Ronald Tree set up the Barbados Museum Collections Fund in May 1951 with $150,000 (museum history page).
- No reporting found of the current size of the government grant.
- No reporting found of any corporate sponsor, named current donor list or published accounts.

CRITICISM IN ART MEDIA
Overall: No criticism of the museum's funding found in art media. The criticism found concerns how its permanent displays treated slavery, and it comes from academic and opinion writing; art-press coverage of its current shows is admiring.
1. ICOFOM Study Series: Natalie A. McGuire, "Developing museological methodologies for unpacking colonial legacies in Barbados" (2024)
URL: https://journals.openedition.org/iss/5985
- The museum's interpretive narrative largely overlooked enslavement until the 1980s, though it had collected slavery-related objects earlier.
- The permanent exhibit set up in 1985 to address the subject "focuses primarily on the period following the Emancipation Act of 1834", with little on resistance, rebellion or the lives of enslaved people beyond the plantation system.
- An academic journal, not art press; names the society directly.
2. Barbados Today: Peter Laurie, "Why we need a museum of slavery in Barbados" (2019)
URL: https://barbadostoday.bb/2019/07/06/why-we-need-a-museum-of-slavery-in-barbados/
- Credits the museum for cataloguing features of enslavement and sponsoring events, but argues its space for slavery is not enough and calls for a dedicated museum.
- Opinion piece by a retired permanent secretary and former ambassador, not art press; criticises the scale of provision, not the museum's money.
3. Studio International: Catherine Mason, "Barbados Museum & Historical Society challenges narrative around slavery" (2026)
URL: https://www.studiointernational.com/index.php/harriet-thomas-weekes-right-to-opacity-joscelyn-gardner-am-i-a-bad-girl-nanny-barbados-museum
- Not criticism of the institution: reviews the Harriet Thomas Weekes and Joscelyn Gardner exhibitions (27 February to 3 October 2026) and calls the museum "an exemplar" in its approach.
- Says archives and museology themselves have origins in "western colonial praxis"; this is a general point, not aimed at the museum.

BOTTOM LINE
The money behind the museum is public and member money with no flagged funder found. The weak points are its dependence on a government grant whose size is not published, five government-appointed Council seats, and no current donor list or accounts found. Worth watching is how its slavery displays develop, since the only criticism found concerns how thinly its permanent exhibit treated that history.
